So far you've met the ESP32 and learned its pin map. Before you write a single line of code, your hands need to learn the tools: placing parts correctly on a breadboard, using a multimeter to see what voltage is really where, soldering the pins onto a module, and powering the board so it doesn't burn out. This lesson is a "workshop": read it at your workbench and do each step as you go.

Why before programming?

The experience of thousands of beginners shows that most "my program doesn't work" problems aren't program problems at all: a wire in the wrong row, a backwards LED, a broken jumper wire, or a board that keeps resetting because of a bad cable. If you nail these skills now, in the chapters ahead your time goes into learning, not into hunting for a loose wire.

A close look at the breadboard: which holes are connected?

A breadboard is a plastic board full of holes with springy metal clips underneath. When you push a component leg or the end of a wire into a hole, the metal clip grips it. Each clip connects several holes together. That's the whole secret of the breadboard: knowing which holes share a clip. With no solder and no tools, you can build a circuit and change it in seconds.

An everyday example: a power strip

Think of each 5-hole column on the breadboard as a tiny power strip: whatever you plug into one of its 5 sockets is connected to the other four. But two power strips sitting side by side have nothing to do with each other until you join them with a wire. The long power rails along the edges are like very long power strips set aside for "power" and "ground".

  • 5-hole strips: in every numbered column, holes a b c d e are connected together, and separately, f g h i j are connected together. An 830-hole board has 63 columns, which means 126 independent 5-hole strips.
  • The center channel: separates the top and bottom halves. It's there so DIP chips (and boards like the ESP32) can straddle it with every pin on its own strip.
  • Power rails: two pairs of long rows along the edges, marked + (usually a red line) and − (a blue line). These are connected horizontally, along the length of the board, not vertically.
  • Split rails: on some long breadboards the rails are cut in the middle of the board (sometimes the red and blue lines also have a break there). If you're not sure, check with the multimeter's beep test (you'll learn it below) and, if needed, join the two halves with a short wire.
The most common breadboard mistake

Putting two legs of the same part in one strip. For example, if both LED legs are in column 14, its two ends are connected together (a short circuit) and the current flows around it; the LED never lights. The rule: every leg of a part goes in its own strip, unless you want to connect them on purpose.

The big problem: the DevKitC doesn't fit on a breadboard

On a standard breadboard, row a to row j spans about 1.1 inches. The two pin rows of the 38-pin DevKitC are exactly 1 inch (25.4 mm) apart, which is 10 holes. If you put one pin row in a, the other lands in i, and the body of the board covers every hole in between. The result: one side of the board has no free holes at all, and the other side has just one. The 30-pin DOIT board is narrower (about 0.9 inch) and usually leaves one free hole on each side, which is still very few.

Jumper wires: male, female and the "short and colorful" rule

  • Keep them short: a long wire picks up noise like an antenna, snags on things and gets lost in the clutter. For permanent connections on the breadboard itself, short pre-bent solid wires (a jumper wire kit) are best.
  • Stick to one color code: red = power (3V3 or 5V), black = GND, other colors = signals. It's the same convention you'll see in every wiring diagram in this course.
  • Throw suspicious wires away: cheap jumper wires are sometimes broken inside the insulation. If a circuit doesn't work, check the wire with the beep test (below). Don't put a bad wire back in the box; cut it right there.

A complete example: the lesson 2.2 diagram on a breadboard, hole by hole

In lesson 2.2 (your first program) you'll build the circuit "GPIO4 → 220 Ω resistor → LED → GND": the status LED that stays on GPIO4 for the whole course. A wiring diagram only tells you what connects to what; here you learn to translate it into exact hole positions. The method is always the same: for every "node" in the circuit (a point where several things meet), pick a separate 5-hole strip.

  1. Count the nodes. Node 1: GPIO4 and the first end of the resistor. Node 2: the second end of the resistor and the LED's anode. Node 3: the LED's cathode and GND. Three nodes means three separate strips: columns 10, 14 and 15.
  2. The resistor: one leg in c10 and the other in c14. A resistor has no polarity.
  3. The LED: the long leg (anode) in d14 (the same strip as the resistor's second end) and the short leg (cathode) in d15.
  4. Ground: a short black wire from a15 to the top − rail, and another black wire from the − rail to one of the GND pins on the ESP32 board.
  5. Signal: an orange wire from pin 4 (GPIO4) on the ESP32 board to a10.
  6. A final check with your finger: trace the path from GPIO4: a10 → (strip 10) → c10 → resistor → c14 → (strip 14) → d14 → LED → d15 → (strip 15) → a15 → − rail → GND. If you had to "jump" anywhere along this chain, that's where the mistake is.
A pro habit: run the rails first

Start every circuit with two wires: the board's 3V3 to the + rail and the board's GND to the − rail. After that, any part that needs power or ground takes it from the nearest rail with a short wire. The circuit ends up tidier and easier to troubleshoot. Just remember to mark whether the + rail is 3.3 V or 5 V (for example, with a strip of masking tape); mixing the two up is one way to burn out an ESP32.

Components 101: resistors, LEDs, capacitors and diodes

The resistor color code

Through-hole resistors are so small that there's no room to print a number on them; instead they have several colored bands. Each color is a digit (black 0 through white 9). On a 4-band resistor, the first two bands are two digits, the third band is the "multiplier" (how many zeros to put after those two digits), and the fourth band (usually gold) is the accuracy, or "tolerance".

One sentence to remember the color order

The colors follow the order of the rainbow, closed off at each end by darkness and light: black (0), brown (1), then the rainbow red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet (2 to 7), and finally gray (8) and white (9). In other words: "out of the dark, through the rainbow, into the light".

Do the math: three examples, step by step

220 Ω (for LEDs): red = 2, red = 2, brown = one zero → 22 + 0 = 220Ω.

10 kΩ (for pull-ups and voltage dividers): brown = 1, black = 0, orange = three zeros → 10 + 000 = 10000Ω = 10kΩ.

4.7 kΩ (a common pull-up for I2C and the DHT22): yellow = 4, violet = 7, red = two zeros → 47 + 00 = 4700Ω = 4.7kΩ.

5-band: the first three bands are digits, the fourth is the multiplier, and the fifth (usually brown = ±1%) is the tolerance. For example, 10 kΩ: brown 1, black 0, black 0, red two zeros → 100 + 00 = 10000Ω.

Which end do I read from? The gold or silver band always comes last, so start from the opposite end. On blue-bodied 5-band resistors, where it's hard to tell, the tolerance band usually sits a little farther from the others. If you're still unsure, there's a simple way to be certain: measure it with the multimeter (you'll learn how below). Brown, red and orange are easy to mix up under warm yellow room light.

Why 220 and 4.7k, not 200 and 5k? (the E12 series)

Factories don't make every possible value. The standard values in the E12 series are these 12 numbers and their multiples of ten: 10 12 15 18 22 27 33 39 47 56 68 82. The reason is simple: with a ±10% tolerance, these numbers are spaced so that any value you want is at most about 10% away from one of them. So 220, 2.2k, 22k, 470, 4.7k and 47k are all "standard". When your math lands on 200 Ω, pick the nearest standard value (220).

Resistor power rating: the ordinary resistors in kits are ¼ watt (0.25 W). Check with the formula P = V × I: the 220 Ω resistor in our LED circuit produces 1.3V × 0.006A ≈ 0.008W of heat, about 30 times less than its limit. In this whole course, the resistors never come close to their power limit; but if a resistor ever gets hot, the current is far higher than you calculated and you have a short circuit somewhere.

Parts with a direction: LEDs, electrolytic capacitors and diodes

Resistors and ceramic capacitors have no polarity; they work whichever way round you put them. But some parts do have polarity, and if you connect them backwards, they either don't work (an LED) or they get damaged (an electrolytic capacitor).

A capacitor is like a small water tank next to the tap

Imagine your building's water comes from far away through a narrow pipe. Every time you suddenly open several taps at once, the pressure drops for a moment. If there's a small tank next to each tap, the tank covers that first moment until the pipe catches up. A capacitor does exactly that: placed next to a part that suddenly draws a lot of current (the ESP32 while transmitting Wi-Fi, a servo as it starts moving), it stores energy and smooths out the brief voltage dip.

  • 100 nF ceramic (decoupling capacitor): small and fast; goes close to the power pin of each module or chip, for very fast noise. Most ready-made modules already have this capacitor on the board.
  • 10 to 100 µF electrolytic (reservoir, or bulk, capacitor): larger and slower; for bigger dips. For example, a 100 µF capacitor across the + and − of a servo's supply, or 10 to 47 µF between 3V3 and GND if the board resets when Wi-Fi starts. The capacitor's voltage rating must be higher than the circuit voltage (for 5 V, use a 10 V or 16 V capacitor).

The multimeter: your eyes for seeing electricity

You can't see electricity. A multimeter is your only way to see instead of guess: is this pin really at 3.3 V? Is this wire intact? Is this resistor really 220 Ω? A cheap multimeter (such as a DT-830 series model or a small UNI-T) is enough for this whole course.

Safety: never go near mains power

Wall-outlet power (mains voltage: 120 V AC in North America, 230 V AC in Europe) can kill. In this course you never need to touch the multimeter probes to a wall outlet, household wiring or the inside of an opened power adapter. All our measurements are direct voltage (DC) below 12 V. If a project (a relay, for example) deals with mains power, only an experienced electrician should do that part.

Measuring voltage on the board's 3V3 and 5V pins

  1. Leads: black in COM, red in VΩ (the jack marked V for voltage).
  2. Selector: on V⎓ (a solid line over a dashed line = DC). If your multimeter is manual-ranging (not auto-ranging), choose the 20 V range: the smallest range larger than 5 volts.
  3. Power the board: plug in the USB cable. No other parts are needed.
  4. Black probe on a GND pin of the board. (To make life easier, hold the black probe on GND with a male-to-female jumper wire or an alligator clip.)
  5. Red probe on 3V3: you should see about 3.25 to 3.35 volts.
  6. Red probe on 5V (on the DOIT: VIN): about 4.7 to 5.1 volts. Being a bit under 5 is normal: the USB cable drops some voltage, and so does the protection diode or fuse on the board. If it's below 4.5 volts, the cable or the USB port is weak.
Seeing a negative number?

If the display shows -3.29, nothing bad has happened: you've just swapped the probes. A digital multimeter in DC mode isn't harmed by swapped probes; it just adds a minus sign. That's a handy trick in itself: the sign tells you which point is more positive.

Don't let the probe slip between neighboring pins

The board's pins are 2.54 mm apart. If the tip of the red probe slips and touches 5V and a neighboring GPIO at the same time, you've made the very mistake of "5 volts on a GPIO". Place the probe from above with a steady hand, or better: measure from the breadboard rail or a jumper wire.

Continuity test (the beep): jumper wires and breadboard strips

The •))) mode (on some models it shares a position with the diode symbol) beeps when the resistance between the two probes is very low (usually below a few tens of ohms). It's the most useful mode for troubleshooting. The golden rule: only on a powered-off circuit (USB unplugged), because this mode sends a little current into the circuit itself.

  1. Test the multimeter itself: touch the two probe tips together. It should beep. If it doesn't, the selector or the jack is wrong.
  2. A jumper wire: one probe on one end, the other probe on the other end. Beep = good. Silence = the wire is broken; throw it away. Bend the wire a little and see whether the beep cuts in and out (a half-broken wire is the worst kind of fault).
  3. A breadboard strip: put two short pieces of solid wire in a5 and e5 and touch the probes to them: beep. Now a5 and f5: silence (the center channel). Now the two ends of the negative rail: if it's silent, your board's rail is split in the middle.
  4. An important test before every time you plug in USB: one probe on 3V3, one on GND. It must not beep continuously. A very short "blip" right at the start is normal (the board's capacitors are charging); but a continuous beep means a short circuit. Don't plug in the USB until you find it.

Measuring resistance (out of the circuit)

  1. Take the resistor out of the circuit. If it's connected to other parts on the breadboard, the multimeter reads the combined resistance of all the parallel paths, not just that one resistor.
  2. Selector on Ω. On a manual-ranging multimeter, pick a range a little larger than the value you expect: for 220 Ω, the 2000 range (not 200; because 220 is more than 200, the display would only show 1 or OL, meaning "over range").
  3. Don't hold both legs with your fingers. Your body is a resistor too (hundreds of kilohms), and it ends up in parallel with large resistors and makes the reading too low. Put the legs in two breadboard holes and touch the probes to them.
  4. Compare the result with the tolerance: a 220 Ω ±5% resistor is fine if it reads anything between 209 and 231.

Measuring current (in series) and mistake number 1

You measure voltage "across" a part (in parallel); the circuit stays as it is. But current has to flow through the multimeter, so you must open the circuit and put the multimeter in the gap (in series). It's just like a water meter: you have to cut the pipe and fit the meter in between.

  1. Disconnect the power and remove the wire whose current you want to know (for example, the wire between 3V3 and the resistor).
  2. Move the red lead to the mA jack (for currents above 200 mA, the 10A jack). Selector on A⎓, range 200m.
  3. Red probe on the side coming from the source (3V3), black probe on the side going to the resistor. Connect the power. For our LED circuit you should see about 6 mA.
  4. Right after you're done, disconnect the power, move the red lead back to the VΩ jack and set the selector to V⎓.
Mistake number 1: probe in the A jack, then measuring voltage

Inside the multimeter, between the mA (or 10A) jack and COM, there's only a very small resistor (a fraction of an ohm to a few ohms); in other words, as far as the circuit is concerned, a multimeter in current mode is practically a bare wire. If the red lead is still in the mA jack and you put the probes on 5V and GND "to measure the voltage", you've effectively created a short circuit: the multimeter's internal fuse blows (if you're lucky), or the USB port and the board's regulator get damaged. On many cheap multimeters the 10A jack has no fuse, and there even the probe leads get hot. Make it a habit: before every measurement, glance at where the red lead is plugged in.

If the multimeter shows a strange number or zero

Suspect the fuse first: if you ever went onto a voltage by mistake while in current mode, the mA fuse has blown and current mode no longer works (voltage and resistance usually still work). A spare fuse with the same rating printed next to the jack (for example 200mA/250V) is cheap.

Soldering a pin header onto a module

Many modules (an OLED display, a BME280 sensor, a GPS module) come with a loose pin header in the bag: a strip of metal pins that you have to solder into the module's holes yourself. Just pushing the pins through the holes without solder doesn't work: the connection is loose and the data cuts in and out. The good news: soldering a pin header is the easiest kind of soldering, and you'll learn it with 10 minutes of practice.

Solder isn't like hot glue; it's like welding

A common mistake is to melt solder on the iron's tip and "smear" it onto the pin like glue. Solder only bonds properly when the pin itself and the pad itself are hot enough to melt it; just as in welding, both metals have to be hot, not only the electrode. So the iron's job is to heat the metals, and you feed the solder to the hot metal, not to the iron's tip.

  1. Preparation: switch the iron on and wait for it to heat up (one to two minutes). Clean the tip and melt a little solder onto it so it shines ("tinning the tip"). A shiny tip transfers heat much better.
  2. The breadboard trick: push the pin header strip (if it's longer than you need, snap it with pliers to the number of pins on the module) into the breadboard with the long side of the pins down. Set the module on top, over the short side of the pins, with the component side facing up (usually the side with the pin labels).
  3. The first pin: place the iron's tip so it touches the pin and the pad at the same time. Wait 1 to 2 seconds. Feed the solder wire from the opposite side to where the pin meets the pad (not to the iron's tip). Add enough solder to cover the pad (about 1 to 2 mm of solder wire).
  4. What to remove first: take the solder wire away first, then the iron. The whole job, heating time included, takes 2 to 3 seconds per pin. Don't move the module for a few seconds, so the solder can set.
  5. Check it's level: you've soldered only one corner pin; now look whether the module sits flat or tilted. If it's tilted, reheat that one pin and straighten the module. Then solder the pin in the opposite corner. Now the module is locked in place.
  6. The remaining pins: one at a time, the same way. Wipe the iron's tip every few pins.
  7. Inspection: under good light (or with your phone camera zoomed in), compare every joint with the picture below. Then use the beep test to make sure no two neighboring pins are connected.

Soldering safety

The iron's tip is about 350 °C and stays hot for several minutes after you switch it off. Always put it back in its stand, never on the table. The smoke that rises is flux vapor (not lead), and it irritates your lungs and eyes: open a window or set up a small fan to blow the smoke away from your face, and don't lean directly over the smoke. Wear glasses; hot solder sometimes spits. If you use leaded solder, wash your hands after you finish and don't eat while soldering.

Powering your circuit: where does the power come from, and where does it go?

In lesson 1.3 you met the 3V3, 5V and GND pins. Now let's follow the whole path from USB to the ESP32 so you understand what each pin is for and how big your current budget is.

  • USB → 5V: the 5 volts from the USB cable (usually through a protection diode or fuse) goes straight to the 5V pin (called VIN on the DOIT).
  • 5V → regulator → 3V3: a linear regulator (an LDO) such as the AMS1117-3.3 (on some boards an SGM2212 or similar) turns the 5 volts into a steady 3.3 volts. Its output feeds both the ESP32 module and the 3V3 pin.
  • The 3V3 pin: for powering 3.3 V sensors and modules.
  • The 5V pin: has two uses: either it's a 5 V output from USB for low-power 5 V modules, or it's an input: when USB isn't connected, you can feed a regulated 5 V supply into this pin (and GND) to power the board.
Do the math: the current budget

On paper, the AMS1117 regulator can deliver up to 1 amp, but a linear regulator turns the extra voltage into heat: (5 − 3.3) × I. At 600 milliamps: 1.7 × 0.6 ≈ 1W of heat in a small part with no heatsink; it gets very hot. That's why, in practice, you should treat about 500 to 600 milliamps as the safe ceiling on most boards (it depends on the regulator and the board design; for your own board, read the name on the three-legged part near the USB connector and look up its datasheet).

The ESP32 itself normally draws about 100 to 150 milliamps with Wi-Fi on, but during radio transmit bursts it pulls up to about 240 milliamps, according to Espressif's datasheet, and the whole board can have even bigger short spikes while connecting to Wi-Fi. So plan for about 250 milliamps for the ESP32 alone, and 500 milliamps for the whole board with some margin. Whatever you feed to sensors from 3V3 comes out of that same share.

Why does a good USB cable matter so much?

Cheap, long cables have very thin wires, which means they have resistance. By Ohm's law, with 500 milliamps and 1 ohm of round-trip resistance: V = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5V of drop. The 5 volts arrives at the board as 4.5, and perhaps lower during a Wi-Fi current spike; the regulator can no longer hold a steady 3.3 volts, and the ESP32 resets with the message Brownout detector was triggered. If your board resets "only when Wi-Fi is on", first try a shorter, thicker cable and a different USB port (or a 2 A wall charger).

Three power rules you never break

  1. One board, one supply. While USB is connected, don't connect another supply to the 5V or 3V3 pin, unless you know exactly where your board has its protection diode. Two supplies at slightly different voltages "fight" each other, and current may flow back into your computer's USB port.
  2. GND is always shared. If a servo or an LED strip gets its power from a separate 5 V supply, that supply's GND must be connected to the ESP32 board's GND. Without a common ground, the GPIO's 3.3 V signal has no reference and the part can't make sense of it. Only the GNDs get connected together, never the +'s.
  3. High-current loads get a separate supply. Power servos, motors, long LED strips and multiple relays from a separate 5 V supply (for example a 5V 2A adapter), with a 100 µF capacitor close to the load. Never from the 3V3 pin.

What about batteries? For portable projects, a 3.7 V lithium battery (with a charger and protection module), a power bank or an AA battery holder are the common choices. Each has its own gotchas (for example, a fully charged lithium battery is at 4.2 V, so it can't go straight to 3V3). You'll see all of this in full, together with deep sleep, in lesson 6.1.

5 V logic versus 3.3 V logic: bring the signal in safely

In lesson 1.1 you saw that 5 volts on a GPIO slowly kills the pin. Here's the exact number from the ESP32 datasheet: the highest allowed input voltage for a logic "one" (VIH max) is VDD + 0.3V; with VDD at 3.3 volts, that's about 3.6 volts. The ESP32's pins are not 5V tolerant. Now you'll learn two practical tools to solve the problem.

Tool 1: the voltage divider (two resistors)

Two resistors in series between the 5 V signal and GND split the voltage between them in proportion to their values. You connect the midpoint to the GPIO. The formula: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2).

Do the math: 10k and 20k

Vout = 5 × 20k ÷ (10k + 20k) = 5 × 0.667 ≈ 3.33V. Perfect: below 3.6 volts, and well above the ESP32's logic "one" threshold (about 0.75 × 3.3 ≈ 2.5V).

The current through the path: 5V ÷ 30kΩ ≈ 0.17mA; very small, so it doesn't load down the signal source. No 20k? Put two 10k resistors in series = 20k. Or use 1k and 2k (or 2.2k): 5 × 2.2 ÷ 3.2 ≈ 3.44V, still safe.

Why values that are neither very small nor very large? Very small ones (say 10 and 20 ohms) waste a lot of current (167 milliamps!). Very large ones (say 1 and 2 megohms) round off the edges of fast signals and pick up noise. The 1k to 20k range is good for ordinary signals.

A voltage divider only works in one direction

A divider only brings a high voltage down, which means it's for a signal traveling from a 5 V module into the ESP32. It doesn't work for lines that are bidirectional (like SDA in I2C) or for very fast signals.

Tool 2: the logic level shifter module

A small, cheap board with 2 or 4 channels, usually built with BSS138 transistors. You connect the LV side to the ESP32 board's 3V3 and the HV side to the module's 5 volts, with the GND of both sides shared, and each LV1↔HV1 channel carries the signal in both directions. That's why it's the standard choice for I2C between an ESP32 and a 5 V module. Its limitation: it's slow for very fast signals (a few megahertz and up); for I2C and ordinary UART it's fine.

When is a 3.3 V signal enough for a 5 V module?

The opposite direction (ESP32 → 5 V module) isn't dangerous; the only question is whether the module understands 3.3 volts as a "one". Every digital input has a threshold called VIH: the lowest voltage that is guaranteed to count as a "one".

  • Modules with TTL-level inputs (like the HC-SR04, most optocoupler relay modules and many motor drivers): VIH is about 2 volts → 3.3 volts is comfortably a "one". That's why TRIG was connected directly in the diagram above.
  • CMOS inputs powered from 5 volts: VIH is usually 0.7 × 5 = 3.5V → 3.3 volts is right on the edge and "might" work. A famous example: WS2812 addressable LEDs powered from 5 volts. Here you need either a level shifter (for example a 74AHCT125 chip) or the tricks you'll see in the relevant lesson.
  • The rule: check the module's datasheet and compare its VIH with 3.3 volts. If you can't find it, add a level shifter; it costs less than a burnt board.

The workbench checklist: before every time you plug in USB

Pilots read a checklist out loud before every flight, even after a thousand flights. You should also do these 6 steps before every time you plug the USB cable into a new or modified circuit. It takes less than a minute.

  1. Beep test 3V3 to GND (USB unplugged): a continuous beep = a short circuit. Find it.
  2. Beep test 5V to GND: same thing.
  3. Rails: is the + rail connected to 3V3 or to 5V? Is every part connected to the right rail? Is there no wire running from 5V to a GPIO?
  4. Polarity: the long legs of the LEDs, the stripes on electrolytic capacitors, the bands on diodes.
  5. Loose wires and bare legs: are there no long, untrimmed legs touching a neighboring leg? No wire half pulled out? Nothing metal (a screw, a coin, a paper clip) under the board?
  6. The first 5 seconds after plugging in USB: keep your hand on the cable. A burning smell, a part heating up fast, or the board's power LED going out = pull the cable immediately.

A mini project with no code: an LED on 3V3, and Ohm's law with a multimeter

Time to practice everything together. No program is needed: we power the LED straight from the 3V3 pin (which is always on), then use the multimeter to test the Ohm's law math from lesson 1.1 in the real world.

  1. Build it: follow the diagram and the exact hole positions section, with one difference: the red wire comes from the 3V3 pin instead of GPIO4.
  2. Run the workbench checklist, then plug in the USB. The LED should light. It doesn't? Turn the LED around.
  3. The 3V3 voltage: multimeter on V⎓, black probe on GND, red on 3V3. Write down the number (for example 3.29).
  4. The resistor voltage (V_R): red probe on c10 (the 3V3 side), black probe on c14. Write down the number (for example 1.34).
  5. The LED voltage (V_LED): red on d14 (anode), black on d15 (cathode). For example 1.95.
  6. Do the math: V_R + V_LED should be roughly equal to the 3V3 voltage: 1.34 + 1.95 = 3.29 ✓. The current: I = V_R ÷ R = 1.34 ÷ 220 ≈ 0.0061A = 6.1mA. That's the same rough 6 milliamps as in exercise 1(c) of lesson 1.1.
  7. Advanced step (optional): measure the resistor's actual value out of the circuit (for example 218 ohms) and redo the math with it. Then measure the current directly with the multimeter in mA mode, in series, and compare it with your calculation. Don't forget to move the red lead back to VΩ.
Why is this experiment worth doing?

Because for the first time you see that the formulas from lesson 1.1 are "real": the source voltage is shared among parts in series (Kirchhoff's voltage law), and you can work out the current from the voltage across a known resistor. That last one is a pro trick: to know the current, you don't have to open the circuit; read the voltage across a known resistor and divide.

Coach's corner

Don't put the multimeter away; from today on it's part of your hand. My rule is simple: don't guess, measure. When a circuit doesn't work, before you change a line of code or ask a question online, measure three things: the 3V3 voltage, the voltage on the part's pin, and the beep along the wire's path. Ninety percent of problems turn up right there. And one more rule: before every measurement, let your eyes go to the red lead's jack first. That half-second glance saves dozens of fuses and a few boards. Really do the exercises in this lesson, not just in your head; hands don't learn by reading.

Lesson quiz

On a breadboard, which two holes are connected to each other?

  1. a5 and a6
  2. a5 and e5
  3. e5 and f5
  4. f5 and j9
In every numbered column, a to e is one strip and f to j is another. a5 and e5 share the same clip.

What is the value of a resistor with yellow, violet, orange and gold bands?

  1. 4.7 kΩ
  2. 473 Ω
  3. 47 kΩ
  4. 74 kΩ
Yellow 4, violet 7, orange ×1000 → 47000Ω = 47kΩ, with a ±5% tolerance.

You just measured the LED current with the multimeter. Right after that you want to check the voltage on the 5V pin. What do you do first?

  1. Move the red lead from the mA jack back to the VΩ jack
  2. Just turn the selector to V⎓
  3. Move the black lead to the 10A jack
  4. Put the probes straight on 5V and GND
In current mode, the multimeter is practically a bare wire. Measuring a voltage with the red lead still in the mA jack = a short circuit and a blown fuse.

You feed a sensor's 5 V output into a GPIO through a divider with R1 = 10k (top) and R2 = 20k (bottom). What is the voltage on the GPIO?

  1. 1.67 volts
  2. 5 volts
  3. 2.5 volts
  4. About 3.33 volts
5 × 20k ÷ 30k ≈ 3.33V: below the allowed limit of VDD + 0.3 ≈ 3.6V and above the logic "one" threshold.

You power a servo from a separate 5 V adapter and connect its signal wire to a GPIO. Which connection is mandatory?

  1. The adapter's + to the board's 5V pin
  2. The adapter's GND to the ESP32 board's GND
  3. The adapter's + to 3V3
  4. None; the signal wire alone is enough
A common ground: a signal voltage only means something relative to a shared GND. The +'s stay separate.

Before plugging in USB, the beep test between 3V3 and GND gives a very short "blip" and then goes quiet. What does that mean?

  1. I have a short circuit; I shouldn't plug in USB
  2. The multimeter is broken
  3. It's normal: the board's capacitors charged up
  4. The regulator is burnt out
The capacitors on the board act like a short circuit for the first instant, then they charge up. Only a continuous beep is a cause for concern.

Exercises

Exercise 1: your breadboard's map

Use the multimeter's beep test to discover your own breadboard's connection map: (a) Are the + and − rails continuous along the whole length, or split in the middle? (b) Are the top and bottom rails connected to each other? (c) Test five random jumper wires; how many were good?

Hint

Use two short pieces of solid wire in the holes as "helper probes", because a probe tip pushed into a breadboard hole either doesn't reach the clip or stretches it. For a rail, put one wire in the first hole of the rail and one in the last.

Answer

The answer depends on your breadboard, and that's the whole point of the exercise: don't assume the map, measure it. (b) is almost always "no"; the top and bottom rails are separate until you join them with a wire. If your rails were split in the middle, mark the breadboard with a marker pen so you remember next time.

Exercise 2: the resistor box

Take five different resistors from your kit. For each one: (1) read the value from the colors and write it down, (2) measure it with the multimeter, (3) check whether it's within the tolerance range. Then build a voltage divider from two 10k resistors, connect its ends to 3V3 and GND, and predict and then measure the voltage at the midpoint.

Hint

For the divider: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). With two equal resistors, what does the fraction come to? Measure Vin with the multimeter too, instead of assuming 3.3.

Answer

With two 10k resistors: Vout = Vin × 10k ÷ 20k = Vin ÷ 2. If your board's 3V3 is at 3.29 volts, the midpoint should be about 1.64 volts. A difference of a few hundredths of a volt is normal (resistor tolerance and multimeter accuracy).

Exercise 3: design on paper (no wiring)

You have a 5 V sensor module with a 5 V digital output (OUT), and you want to connect it to GPIO33. You only have 1k and 2.2k resistors. (a) Which resistor goes on top and which on the bottom? Calculate the GPIO voltage. (b) Where do you power the sensor and the ESP32 from? (c) Write down the exact positions of the parts on the breadboard (by hole letter and number).

Hint

The GPIO connects to the midpoint, and its voltage equals the voltage across the bottom resistor. Which one do you want to be larger to get to about 3.3? You have three nodes: OUT, the midpoint and GND.

Answer

(a) R1 = 1k on top (between OUT and the midpoint), R2 = 2.2k on the bottom (between the midpoint and GND): 5 × 2.2 ÷ 3.2 ≈ 3.44V; below 3.6, so it's safe. The other way round you'd get 5 × 1 ÷ 3.2 ≈ 1.56V, which is below the "one" threshold (about 2.5 volts) and wouldn't work. (b) The sensor's VCC from the board's 5V pin, the sensor's GND to the board's GND (shared), and the ESP32 from USB. (c) For example: the OUT wire to a20, R1 from c20 to c24, R2 from d24 to d28, a blue wire from a24 to GPIO33, a black wire from a28 to the − rail.

The lesson in three lines

A breadboard has 5-hole strips separated by the center channel; use one strip per circuit node, and two breadboards for the wide DevKitC. Only use the multimeter with the red lead in the right jack: voltage in parallel, current in series, the beep test only on a powered-off circuit. The ESP32's pins tolerate up to about 3.6 volts; bring 5 V signals in through a divider or a level shifter, and power high-current loads from a separate supply with a shared GND.
