This lesson starts at "what is a network?" and ends with a professional, self-healing connection.

Networking from zero: the router, IP and DHCP

Before any code, you should know exactly what happens when we say "the ESP32 connected to Wi-Fi". Don't worry; it's only a handful of simple ideas.

What is a network?

A network is a group of devices that can send messages to each other. At home, your phone, laptop and TV are probably all on the same network. In the middle of that network sits a box we usually call "the modem". That box actually does two jobs:

  • Modem: connects your phone line, fiber or SIM card to the internet. It is the part that talks to your internet provider (ISP).
  • Router: builds the network inside your home, gives every device an address, and moves messages between the devices and the internet. The Wi-Fi antennas belong to this part.

In most homes both jobs happen in one box (for example an ADSL/VDSL modem or a 4G modem). In this course, whenever we say "modem" or "router", we mean that box.

An everyday example: an apartment building

Picture your home network as an apartment building. The router is the front-desk doorman. Every apartment (every device) has an apartment number (its local IP address). To the outside world, the whole building has just one street address (the public IP). When a letter arrives from outside, it goes to the doorman, and the doorman knows which apartment it belongs to. The fixed serial number on each apartment's door is the MAC address: it never changes, even if the tenant moves to another apartment.

IP address: every device's apartment number

Every device on the network has an IP address: four numbers from 0 to 255 separated by dots, like 192.168.1.37. Every message that travels on the network carries the sender's and receiver's addresses, just like an envelope.

Addresses that start with 192.168. or 10. or 172.16. through 172.31. are always local. This matters in the next lesson: when the ESP32 runs a web server, its address is local and can't be reached directly from outside your home.

DHCP: who hands out the apartment numbers?

You never told your phone to use the IP 192.168.1.23. The router runs a service called DHCP that automatically "lends" a free IP to every device that joins. The loan has a time limit (say, 24 hours), which is why your board's IP might be slightly different tomorrow.

With numbers: the four DHCP steps

The ESP32 powers up and shouts: "Can anyone give me an IP?" (Discover). The router answers: "192.168.1.37 is free" (Offer). The ESP32 says: "I'll take that one" (Request). The router confirms: "It's yours for 86400 seconds" (Ack). All of this usually takes less than a second. In our code, the moment this step finishes is the GOT_IP event.

Want the board to always have the same IP?

The easiest and cleanest way is in the router: most routers have a "DHCP reservation" (or "static lease") page where you pick the ESP32 by its MAC address and give it a fixed number. Your code stays unchanged. The alternative is to set it in code, before WiFi.begin: WiFi.config(IPAddress(192,168,1,50), IPAddress(192,168,1,1), IPAddress(255,255,255,0), IPAddress(192,168,1,1)); (the address, the gateway, the subnet mask and a DNS server). Pick a number outside the range the router hands out by DHCP, or two devices may end up with the same address. In lesson 4.2 you'll also meet mDNS, which lets you reach the board by name, so the number matters less.

SSID, password and MAC

  • SSID is the name of the Wi-Fi network; it's what you see in your phone's Wi-Fi list (for example Home or Home_5G). It is case-sensitive: Home and home are two different networks.
  • Password: on WPA2 networks it must be 8 to 63 characters long. The ESP32 compares it exactly, character by character.
  • MAC address is a 6-byte ID written into the chip at the factory, like 24:0A:C4:12:34:56. The router uses it to tell devices apart. Some routers only let in "allowed" MAC addresses (MAC filtering).

2.4 GHz: the biggest beginner trap

Wi-Fi works on two frequency "bands": 2.4 GHz (longer range, lower speed, goes through walls better) and 5 GHz (higher speed, shorter range). The ESP32 supports only 2.4 GHz (the 802.11 b/g/n standard). That means it doesn't see a 5 GHz network at all; it's not that it sees it and fails to connect.

Careful: 5 GHz networks don't show up in the ESP32's scan

Many newer routers create two networks, for example Home and Home_5G. Always connect the ESP32 to the one without 5G. If your router broadcasts both bands under one name (band steering), it's usually fine and the ESP32 joins the 2.4 GHz part; but if the 2.4 GHz band is turned off in the router settings, the ESP32 will never connect. An iPhone hotspot also sometimes switches to 5 GHz; turn on the Maximize Compatibility option. (A rare exception: the new ESP32-C5 chip is dual-band, but this course's board is a regular ESP32.)

RSSI: signal strength as a number

RSSI is the received signal strength, measured in dBm. The numbers are negative, and the closer to zero, the stronger. So −45 is much better than −80. Every 3 dB means half or double the power, so the difference between −60 and −70 is much more than "a little": the power has dropped about 10 times.

RSSIQualityIn practice
-30 to -50ExcellentThe board is right next to the router. No problems at all.
-50 to -60GoodEnough for any project.
-60 to -70FairIt works; sometimes slow or laggy.
-70 to -80WeakOccasional dropouts. Move the board.
Below -80Almost unusableThe connection keeps dropping or never comes up.

The ESP32's three Wi-Fi roles: STA, AP and AP+STA

The ESP32 can play three roles on Wi-Fi:

  • Station (STA): joins your home router just like your phone does. It gets an IP from the router and has internet access. 90% of projects use this mode.
  • Access Point (AP): the ESP32 creates its own Wi-Fi network. Your phone connects straight to the ESP32. There's no internet, but it's great for places with no router (a garden, a workshop) or for a first-time setup page. In this mode the ESP32's own default IP is 192.168.4.1.
  • AP+STA: both at once. It's connected to the router and has its own network at the same time. The WiFiManager library (further down) uses exactly this.

Why STA most of the time?

In STA mode the ESP32 has internet access (time, MQTT, the cloud) and every device in the house can find it. In AP mode, anyone who wants to talk to the board has to leave their own Wi-Fi and join the ESP32's network, which means their phone loses internet. That's why AP is mostly used for "first-time setup" and for places without a router.

This short program puts the ESP32 into AP mode so you can see how simple it is:

#include <WiFi.h>

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_AP);                              // Access Point only
  bool ok = WiFi.softAP("ESP32-Setup", "12345678"); // name and password (at least 8 characters)
  Serial.println(ok ? "AP started" : "AP failed");
  Serial.print("AP IP: ");
  Serial.println(WiFi.softAPIP());                 // usually 192.168.4.1
}

void loop() {
  Serial.printf("Clients connected: %d\n", WiFi.softAPgetStationNum());
  delay(3000);
}
  1. The WiFi library. WiFi.h comes with the ESP32 package; you don't need to install anything from the Library Manager. Everything Wi-Fi (STA, AP, scanning, events) is done with this one library and a ready-made object called WiFi.
  2. WiFi.mode(WIFI_AP). Sets the board's role to "access point only". The other options are WIFI_STA and WIFI_AP_STA. Stating the role explicitly makes the behavior predictable. The ESP32 keeps its previous Wi-Fi settings in memory; if you don't set the role, it might start with the role left over from the last program.
  3. WiFi.softAP(name, password). Creates a network called ESP32-Setup with the password 12345678 and returns true if it succeeds. A WPA2 password must be at least 8 characters. If you give a shorter one, softAP fails. Always print the result so you know when it fails.
  4. WiFi.softAPIP(). The ESP32's own address on the network it created, usually 192.168.4.1. In this mode the ESP32 plays the router itself and runs DHCP too; your phone gets IPs like 192.168.4.2.
  5. Counting guests. softAPgetStationNum() returns the number of connected devices. Join ESP32-Setup with your phone and watch the number go up. A simple test that proves the network really works. By default the ESP32 allows at most 4 guests at the same time.

From here on, this lesson works only with STA mode.

Step 1: see which networks the ESP32 can see (Wi-Fi scanner)

Before connecting, it's smart to make sure the ESP32 can see your network at all. This program lists the networks around you every 10 seconds, just like opening the Wi-Fi list on your phone.

#include <WiFi.h>                 // built-in Wi-Fi library (nothing to install)

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);            // station mode (like a phone)
  WiFi.disconnect();              // if it was connected somewhere, disconnect
  delay(100);
  Serial.print("My MAC address: ");
  Serial.println(WiFi.macAddress());
}

void loop() {
  Serial.println("Scanning...");
  int n = WiFi.scanNetworks();    // takes about 2 to 4 seconds

  if (n <= 0) {
    Serial.println("No networks found");
  } else {
    Serial.printf("%d networks found:\n", n);
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
      Serial.printf("%2d | %-24s | %4d dBm | ch %2d | %s\n",
                    i + 1,
                    WiFi.SSID(i).c_str(),
                    WiFi.RSSI(i),
                    WiFi.channel(i),
                    WiFi.encryptionType(i) == WIFI_AUTH_OPEN ? "open" : "locked");
    }
  }
  WiFi.scanDelete();              // free the memory used by the scan results
  Serial.println();
  delay(10000);                   // scan again in 10 seconds
}
  1. STA mode, and drop any old connection. To scan, the board must be in station mode. WiFi.disconnect() ends any earlier connection, and delay(100) gives the radio a moment to get ready. If the board is in the middle of trying to connect, the scan may fail or return incomplete results. "Settle down first, then scan."
  2. Print the MAC address. WiFi.macAddress() returns the board's MAC address as text. If your router uses MAC filtering, you'll need to add exactly this value in the router settings. It's a good idea to write it down right at the start.
  3. WiFi.scanNetworks(). Sweeps the radio across all the 2.4 GHz channels and returns how many networks it found. Meanwhile (about 2 to 4 seconds) the program waits. For a troubleshooting tool, waiting is fine. If a negative number comes back (−1 means still scanning, −2 means it failed), we treat it as "nothing found"; that's why the condition is n <= 0, not n == 0.
  4. Print each network on one line. Serial.printf is like print but with a template: %d is a placeholder for a whole number, %s for text, and %-24s means "left-align the text in 24 columns". For each network we print its name (SSID(i)), signal strength (RSSI(i)), channel and whether it's locked. A tidy table is much easier to read than jumbled lines. .c_str() is needed because %s wants C-style text, not a String object. The expression condition ? a : b is a short if: "open" if the network is open, otherwise "locked".
  5. WiFi.scanDelete(). Frees the memory that held the scan results. If you scan in an endless loop and never free that memory, it slowly fills up. Good habit: whatever you take, give back.

The output on a real board looks something like this (in Wokwi you'll only see Wokwi-GUEST):

My MAC address: 24:0A:C4:12:34:56 Scanning... 4 networks found: 1 | Home | -48 dBm | ch 6 | locked 2 | Neighbor-WiFi | -71 dBm | ch 11 | locked 3 | MobinNet-2G | -79 dBm | ch 1 | locked 4 | Cafe-Free | -86 dBm | ch 6 | open
Your first troubleshooting test

If your home network isn't in this list, no code will ever connect to it. The cause is almost always one of these: the network is 5 GHz, the SSID is hidden, or the board is too far away. Fix that first.

Step 2: connecting with a timeout

The simplest connection code you'll find online is this: while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) delay(500);. The problem? If the password is wrong, the board stays in that loop forever and doesn't say a word. We'll write a better version that waits at most 15 seconds and tells you why it failed.

#include <WiFi.h>

const char* WIFI_SSID = "Wokwi-GUEST";    // put your own network name here
const char* WIFI_PASS = "";               // network password (empty for Wokwi)
const unsigned long TIMEOUT_MS = 15000;   // wait at most 15 seconds

// turns the status number into a readable name
const char* statusName(wl_status_t s) {
  switch (s) {
    case WL_IDLE_STATUS:     return "IDLE (0)";
    case WL_NO_SSID_AVAIL:   return "NO_SSID_AVAIL (1)";
    case WL_CONNECTED:       return "CONNECTED (3)";
    case WL_CONNECT_FAILED:  return "CONNECT_FAILED (4)";
    case WL_CONNECTION_LOST: return "CONNECTION_LOST (5)";
    case WL_DISCONNECTED:    return "DISCONNECTED (6)";
    default:                 return "OTHER";
  }
}

bool connectWiFi() {
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);
  Serial.printf("Connecting to %s", WIFI_SSID);

  unsigned long start = millis();
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) {
    if (millis() - start > TIMEOUT_MS) {            // timeout
      Serial.printf("\nFailed! status = %s\n", statusName(WiFi.status()));
      return false;
    }
    Serial.print(".");
    delay(500);
  }
  Serial.println("\nConnected!");
  return true;
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  if (connectWiFi()) {
    Serial.print("IP address : "); Serial.println(WiFi.localIP());
    Serial.print("Gateway    : "); Serial.println(WiFi.gatewayIP());
    Serial.print("MAC address: "); Serial.println(WiFi.macAddress());
    Serial.printf("RSSI       : %d dBm\n", WiFi.RSSI());
  } else {
    Serial.println("Check SSID, password, and that the network is 2.4 GHz");
  }
}

void loop() {
  static unsigned long last = 0;
  if (millis() - last >= 5000) {                    // every 5 seconds
    last = millis();
    Serial.printf("status=%s  RSSI=%d dBm\n", statusName(WiFi.status()), WiFi.RSSI());
  }
}
  1. Network name, password and timeout. const char* means "a fixed piece of text". For Wokwi the name is Wokwi-GUEST and the password is empty; on a real board, write your own network's name and password. unsigned long is the right type for milliseconds. Putting these at the top of the program means you don't have to hunt through the code to change networks. (A bit later you'll learn why it's better not to keep the password in this file at all.)
  2. Turning the status code into a name. WiFi.status() returns a number. This function uses a switch to turn that number into a readable name. "status = 1" tells you nothing; "NO_SSID_AVAIL" means "no network with this name was found". A readable log means fast troubleshooting.
  3. Start connecting. WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA) selects the station role, and WiFi.begin(name, password) starts the connection attempt and returns right away. Key point: begin does not wait for the connection to finish. Connecting carries on in the background, and we have to ask for the status. This is exactly what later lets us write a program that never freezes.
  4. A waiting loop with a limit. We store the start time in start. As long as we're not connected, we print a dot every 500 milliseconds; but if millis() - start goes over 15000, we leave with an error message and the value false. This is the same millis() pattern from lesson 3.1. The dots show the program is alive. The timeout guarantees the board never silently hangs. 15 seconds is plenty: a normal connection usually takes 2 to 5 seconds.
  5. Print the network details. If it connected: the board's own IP (localIP), the router's IP (gatewayIP), the MAC and the signal strength (RSSI). If not, we print a short hint. You'll need the IP in the next lesson to reach the board from a browser. RSSI is the first number to look at when a connection is unstable.
  6. A non-blocking loop. Prints the status and RSSI every 5 seconds. The word static means the variable last is set to zero only once and keeps its value between passes of loop. With this log you can carry the board around the house and see where the signal gets weak: a free "signal meter".
Connecting to Home..... Connected! IP address : 192.168.1.37 Gateway : 192.168.1.1 MAC address: 24:0A:C4:12:34:56 RSSI : -52 dBm status=CONNECTED (3) RSSI=-53 dBm

Get the Wi-Fi password out of your code (the secrets.h pattern)

So far we've written the password straight into the code. Now imagine you put the code on GitHub or send it to a friend on a messaging app so they can help you: your home Wi-Fi password goes along with it! The professional solution is to keep passwords in a separate file that is never published.

An everyday example

You can hand a cake recipe to anyone, but you don't tuck your house key inside the cookbook. The program code is the recipe, and secrets.h is the key you keep separately in your pocket.

  1. Create a new file. In the Arduino IDE, click the small ⋯ menu next to the tab names (top right of the editor) and choose New Tab. Name it exactly secrets.h. The file is saved next to your .ino file in the same folder.
  2. Write your passwords in it:
#pragma once
// Passwords live only here. Never post this file on GitHub or in a group chat.
#define WIFI_SSID "MyHomeWiFi"
#define WIFI_PASS "my-strong-password"
  1. #pragma once. Tells the compiler to read this file only once, even if it gets included several times. Without it, if two different files include secrets.h, you get a "redefinition" error.
  2. #define. Creates a "text substitution": wherever the compiler sees WIFI_SSID, it replaces it with "MyHomeWiFi". The simplest approach for constants that are defined only once. If your password contains a " or a \, you must put a \ in front of it: for example, write the password ab"c as "ab\"c".
  1. In the main program, write #include "secrets.h" (with double quotes, not <>; quotes mean "look for it in the project's own folder").
  2. If you use Git, create a .gitignore file in the project folder and put this line in it so Git never picks up secrets.h:
secrets.h
  1. The name of a file that must not be published. Git ignores every file whose name is listed in .gitignore. So that others know which file to create, people usually publish a sample copy called secrets_example.h with made-up values (below).
#pragma once
// This is the sample copy and goes on GitHub; the real values live in secrets.h
#define WIFI_SSID "YOUR_WIFI_NAME"
#define WIFI_PASS "YOUR_WIFI_PASSWORD"
  1. Made-up values. Anyone who downloads the project copies this file, renames it to secrets.h and fills in their own password. The project stays buildable for everyone, and nobody's password leaks.
The password isn't hidden on the board itself

secrets.h only stops the password from leaking through the source code. The password is still inside the compiled program in the board's flash, and anyone who has the board in their hands can read it (unless you use Flash Encryption, which is an advanced topic). So don't leave the board lying around in a public place.

WiFiManager: set up Wi-Fi from your phone (a quick intro)

If you build a device and give it to someone else, they can't write their home Wi-Fi password into the code and upload it again. The WiFiManager library solves this (in the Library Manager, search for WiFiManager, and among the several results with similar names install exactly WiFiManager by tzapu, version 2.0.17 or newer; it needs no other dependencies). If the board can't connect, it opens a network in AP mode; the user joins it with their phone, a settings page opens automatically (a captive portal, like hotel Wi-Fi), they pick the network and type the password, and the board saves them in its own memory.

#include <WiFiManager.h>              // the tzapu/WiFiManager library

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  WiFiManager wm;
  wm.setConfigPortalTimeout(180);     // wait at most 3 minutes for setup
  // if the saved password works, it connects directly;
  // otherwise it creates a Wi-Fi network called ESP32-Setup and waits for setup from a phone
  bool ok = wm.autoConnect("ESP32-Setup", "setup1234");
  Serial.println(ok ? "Connected!" : "Config portal timed out");
}

void loop() {}
  1. The library and the manager object. WiFiManager wm; creates a Wi-Fi manager. All the complicated work (AP, web page, saving the password) lives inside the library; you only write a few lines.
  2. The portal timeout. If nobody completes the setup within 180 seconds, autoConnect returns false. Without this line the board waits for setup forever: exactly the kind of "freezing" we're fighting in this lesson.
  3. autoConnect. First it tries the saved password; if that fails, it creates the ESP32-Setup network with the password setup1234. Join it with your phone; if the page doesn't open by itself, go to 192.168.4.1 in your browser. This is the AP+STA mode you saw above, in action.

Step 3: a stable connection with automatic reconnect

Now for the most important part of the lesson. In the real world, Wi-Fi drops: the router restarts at night, the power goes out and comes back, someone turns on the microwave (microwave ovens also work at 2.4 GHz!). A good program has three qualities:

  1. It never waits for Wi-Fi. If the board has buttons, sensors or motors besides Wi-Fi, they must keep working even while the network is down.
  2. It comes back by itself. No manual reset needed.
  3. It tells you what happened. It prints the reason for the dropout.
An everyday example: a doorbell instead of checking the door over and over

Say you're waiting for the mail carrier. The bad way: open the door every 10 seconds to check whether they've arrived, and do nothing else in the meantime. The good way: get on with your work and react when the doorbell rings. Wi-Fi "events" are that doorbell: whenever something happens (connected, got an IP, disconnected), the Wi-Fi library calls the function you registered.

Our three tools

  • WiFi.setAutoReconnect(true): tells the library itself to try again if the connection drops. (It's on by default in core 3.x; we write it out anyway to be sure.)
  • WiFi.onEvent(function): registers our function as the "doorbell".
  • A watchdog built with millis(): if we still stay offline for a while despite all that, we give the reconnect a nudge ourselves every 10 seconds, without ever stopping loop.

Wiring diagram: a network status LED

We put an LED on GPIO4 so you can see the status even without the Serial Monitor: steady on = connected, fast blinking = disconnected. The wiring is exactly the same as in lesson 2.2.

The program below needs the secrets.h file you created above:

#include <WiFi.h>
#include <time.h>
#include "secrets.h"                          // WIFI_SSID and WIFI_PASS live here

const int LED_PIN = 4;                        // network status LED
const unsigned long RETRY_MS = 10000;         // one manual retry every 10 seconds
const char* TZ_INFO = "EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0"; // Toronto/New York: put yours

volatile bool wifiUp = false;                 // do we have an IP right now?
unsigned long lastRetry = 0, lastBlink = 0, lastPrint = 0;
bool ledState = false;

// the Wi-Fi library itself calls this function whenever something happens
void onWiFiEvent(WiFiEvent_t event, WiFiEventInfo_t info) {
  switch (event) {
    case ARDUINO_EVENT_WIFI_STA_CONNECTED:
      Serial.println("[WiFi] Linked to router, waiting for IP...");
      break;
    case ARDUINO_EVENT_WIFI_STA_GOT_IP:
      wifiUp = true;
      Serial.print("[WiFi] Got IP: ");
      Serial.println(IPAddress(info.got_ip.ip_info.ip.addr));
      break;
    case ARDUINO_EVENT_WIFI_STA_DISCONNECTED:
      wifiUp = false;
      Serial.printf("[WiFi] Disconnected, reason %d (%s)\n",
                    info.wifi_sta_disconnected.reason,
                    WiFi.disconnectReasonName((wifi_err_reason_t)info.wifi_sta_disconnected.reason));
      break;
    default:
      break;
  }
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);

  WiFi.onEvent(onWiFiEvent);                  // register before begin
  WiFi.setHostname("esp32-lab");              // the board's name in the router's list
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
  WiFi.setAutoReconnect(true);                // reconnect by itself after a dropout
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);           // we don't wait!
  Serial.println("[WiFi] Connecting in background...");

  configTzTime(TZ_INFO, "pool.ntp.org", "time.google.com");
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long now = millis();

  // 1) watchdog: if we're still offline, give a small nudge every 10 seconds
  if (!wifiUp && now - lastRetry >= RETRY_MS) {
    lastRetry = now;
    Serial.println("[WiFi] Still offline -> reconnect()");
    WiFi.disconnect();
    WiFi.reconnect();
  }

  // 2) LED: connected = steady on, disconnected = fast blink
  if (wifiUp) {
    ledState = true;
  } else if (now - lastBlink >= 200) {
    lastBlink = now;
    ledState = !ledState;
  }
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, ledState ? HIGH : LOW);

  // 3) print the time and signal strength every 5 seconds
  if (now - lastPrint >= 5000) {
    lastPrint = now;
    struct tm t;
    if (getLocalTime(&t, 10)) {               // wait at most 10 milliseconds
      char buf[24];
      strftime(buf, sizeof(buf), "%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S", &t);
      Serial.printf("[Time] %s  RSSI %d dBm\n", buf, WiFi.RSSI());
    } else {
      Serial.println("[Time] not synced yet");
    }
  }
}
  1. Three includes. WiFi.h for the network, time.h for working with time and date (the standard C library), and our own secrets.h for the name and password. Because WIFI_SSID and WIFI_PASS are now defined in secrets.h, there's no password in this file anymore. You can publish this file without worrying.
  2. Constants. The LED pin, the retry interval (10 seconds) and the time zone string (here Toronto and New York; you'll put in your own city's string, explained in detail further down). 10 seconds is a good balance: short enough to reconnect soon after the router comes back, long enough not to wear out the router (and ourselves) with back-to-back attempts.
  3. State variables. wifiUp says whether we have an IP right now. The word volatile tells the compiler "this variable may be changed from somewhere else, so really read it from memory every time". The event function runs in a task (a separate thread of execution) apart from loop. volatile prevents loop from keeping a stale copy of the value in the processor's registers and missing the change.
  4. The event function. In core 3.x its signature is exactly (WiFiEvent_t event, WiFiEventInfo_t info). event says what happened and info holds the details. With a switch we react to only the three important events and ignore the rest (default). The library has dozens of events (scan started, AP started, and so on). You don't need to know them all; these three are enough for a station.
  5. STA_CONNECTED. The ESP32 has "shaken hands" with the router and the password was accepted, but it doesn't have an IP yet. The difference between CONNECTED and GOT_IP matters: without an IP you can't send any message yet. If you see this line but GOT_IP never comes, the problem is the router's DHCP, not the password.
  6. STA_GOT_IP: now we're really online. We set wifiUp to true and print the IP from inside info. IPAddress(...) turns the raw number into the familiar 192.168.1.37 form. We tie "being online" to GOT_IP, not to CONNECTED, because only from this moment can data be sent.
  7. STA_DISCONNECTED: we got cut off. We set wifiUp to false and print the reason code (reason) and its name (disconnectReasonName). The reason code is the most valuable troubleshooting information you have (table below). On purpose, we don't call WiFi.begin or delay here: the event function must finish quickly, and calling Wi-Fi functions from inside it has caused strange behavior in reports to Espressif. We leave the heavy work to loop.
  8. Registering the doorbell. WiFi.onEvent(onWiFiEvent) hands our function to the library. This happens before begin. If you register it after begin, the connection might come up so fast that you miss the GOT_IP event, and wifiUp stays false forever.
  9. Hostname, mode, auto-reconnect and start. setHostname is the name you see in the router's device list. setAutoReconnect(true) turns on automatic retries. begin starts connecting and the program carries on without waiting. Instead of "esp32-A1B2C3" you see "esp32-lab" in the router's list and can find your device easily. The hostname must be set before mode and begin, otherwise it isn't applied. And more importantly: there's no waiting loop in setup; the board enters loop within the first second.
  10. Starting NTP. configTzTime sets the time zone and two time servers. From then on the ESP32 fetches the time from the internet by itself, in the background (as soon as the connection is up), and re-syncs once every hour. We call it after WiFi.mode so the network stack (TCP/IP) is already running. We give two servers so that if one doesn't answer, the other does. Full explanation in the NTP section.
  11. The watchdog. If we're offline and 10 seconds have passed since the last attempt, we call disconnect() and then reconnect(). Both functions return immediately. Auto-reconnect is enough in most cases, but in some situations (for example a router that has been off for a long time) the library gives up trying. This watchdog guarantees the board never stays offline "forever". Because it only runs once every 10 seconds, loop still spins freely thousands of times per second.
  12. A status LED without delay. Connected: the LED is steady on. Disconnected: the LED flips every 200 milliseconds (2.5 blinks per second). This part proves loop never freezes: even while the network is down, the steady blinking carries on. If there were a long delay or a waiting loop anywhere in the program, the blinking would stop. We keep track of ledState ourselves instead of reading the pin back; that's simpler and more reliable.
  13. Printing the time every 5 seconds. getLocalTime(&t, 10) fills the t structure with the current time. The 10 means "wait at most 10 milliseconds". strftime turns the time into text using the pattern %Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S. By default getLocalTime waits up to 5000 milliseconds, which means that when there's no internet, loop freezes for 5 seconds every time and the LED stops blinking! With the 10 we removed that trap. If the time hasn't synced yet, it returns false and we print "not synced yet".

Exact time from the internet: NTP and your time zone

The ESP32 has no battery-backed clock. Every time it powers up, it thinks it's 1970! But when it's online, it can ask NTP servers (Network Time Protocol) for the exact world time. pool.ntp.org is a free, worldwide pool of thousands of time servers.

An everyday example

It's like setting your wristwatch by the time announcement on the radio. The radio announces Greenwich time (UTC), and you have to know how far your city is from it, and whether it's summer time right now. NTP is the radio, and the "time zone string" is that knowledge, written down once.

NTP always delivers UTC. To turn it into local time, the ESP32 needs a time zone string in the POSIX format. It looks cryptic, but it's only a few pieces. Take Toronto and New York, EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0:

PieceMeaning
ESTThe name of standard (winter) time; for display only.
5The offset from UTC. The sign is reversed! In the POSIX format it means "how much you must add to local time to get UTC". Toronto is 5 hours behind UTC, so you add 5: a plain 5. Berlin is 1 hour ahead, so it's written -1.
EDTThe name of daylight saving (summer) time. Just having it means "this place has summer time, one hour ahead".
M3.2.0When summer time starts: Month 3 (March), week 2, day 0 (Sunday), so "the second Sunday of March", at 2:00 by default.
M11.1.0When it ends: the first Sunday of November. A /3 after a rule (as in Berlin's M10.5.0/3) means "at 3:00" instead of 2:00.

The big win: once the string is right, the ESP32 switches between winter and summer time by itself, on the right night, every year. Some common strings:

PlaceTime zone string
Toronto, New York, MontrealEST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0
Chicago, WinnipegCST6CDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0
Denver, CalgaryMST7MDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0
Vancouver, Los Angeles, SeattlePST8PDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0
LondonGMT0BST,M3.5.0/1,M10.5.0
Berlin, Paris, Madrid, Rome, AmsterdamCET-1CEST,M3.5.0,M10.5.0/3
Tehran (no daylight saving time since 2022)<+0330>-3:30
UTC itself (no time zone)UTC0
How to find your own string

Search for your city (for example America/Toronto or Europe/Berlin) in the zones.csv list of the posix_tz_db project and copy the string next to it. On a Mac or Linux computer you can also read it from the system: tail -n 1 /usr/share/zoneinfo/America/Toronto prints exactly this format. Then put it in TZ_INFO in the code above.

Careful: the two most common mistakes

1) The reversed sign: writing EST-5... for Toronto puts your clock 10 hours off. 2) A string without the summer-time part (just EST5) is correct all winter and one hour off all summer. You'll also see configTime(-5 * 3600, 3600, "pool.ntp.org"); in tutorials: a fixed offset in seconds plus a fixed one-hour DST. It can't know when summer time starts and ends, so configTzTime with a full string is the better habit.

Reading the pieces of the time: struct tm

getLocalTime(&t) fills a struct tm: a small box with one drawer for each part of the date and time. You read a drawer with a dot: t.tm_hour.

FieldRangeWatch out
tm_hour, tm_min, tm_sec0–23, 0–59, 0–5924-hour clock: 10 p.m. is 22
tm_mday1–31Day of the month; the only field that starts at 1
tm_mon0–11January is 0! Add 1 before printing
tm_yearyears since 19002026 is stored as 126; add 1900
tm_wday0–60 = Sunday, 6 = Saturday
struct tm t;
if (getLocalTime(&t, 10)) {
  if (t.tm_hour == 22 && t.tm_min == 0) {
    Serial.println("It's 10 p.m.: time for the night light");
  }
  Serial.printf("Date: %d-%02d-%02d\n", t.tm_year + 1900, t.tm_mon + 1, t.tm_mday);
}
  1. Fill the box. struct tm t; makes an empty box, and getLocalTime fills it. It returns false if the time hasn't been synced yet. The & hands the function the box's address so it can write into it (you'll meet & properly in lesson 4.3). The 10 means "wait at most 10 ms", as in the program above.
  2. A schedule. "If it's 22:00" is simply two comparisons. This check is true for the whole minute 22:00, so in a real project also remember that you already did the job today (a bool), or it runs thousands of times in that minute.
  3. Printing a date. Year + 1900, month + 1; %02d pads with a zero (09). These two "+" are the classic struct tm traps. For a whole formatted string, strftime (in the program above) does it all for you.
Why does exact time matter?

To timestamp sensor readings (logging temperature to an SD card), to switch on the yard light at exactly 7 p.m., and so that the logs and messages you send to the internet in chapter 5 (MQTT, HTTP) carry the real time. One subtle point: on many devices and libraries, a secure connection (HTTPS or MQTT over TLS) checks the certificate's validity dates against the device's clock and fails without the correct time. In Arduino-ESP32 core 3.3, WiFiClientSecure does not check the certificate dates by default, so the connection works even without NTP; still, make getting the time a habit, because other libraries and chips may be stricter, and a log without timestamps is useless for troubleshooting. (HTTPS details are in lesson 5.6.)

Enough power: why does the board reset when Wi-Fi turns on?

The Wi-Fi radio draws a lot of current while transmitting: up to about 250 mA in short pulses, while without Wi-Fi about 40 to 50 mA is enough. If the power supply is weak (a thin, long USB cable, an unpowered USB hub port, a cheap power bank), the 3.3 V rail dips for a moment (you saw the power path from USB to the regulator in lesson 1.4), and a built-in protection circuit (the Brownout Detector) resets the board to prevent erratic behavior.

Brownout detector was triggered ets Jun 8 2016 00:22:57 rst:0xc (SW_CPU_RESET),boot:0x13 (SPI_FAST_FLASH_BOOT)
With numbers

A cheap 1.5 m USB cable can have about 1 Ω of total resistance (there and back). At 0.25 A: V = I × R = 0.25 × 1 = 0.25V of drop. So the port's 5 V arrives as 4.75 V, plus the regulator's own drop; and if the computer's port is weak to begin with, that's enough to push the voltage below the safe limit for a moment.

  • Use a short, thick cable and a USB port directly on the computer, or a 5 V charger rated 1 A or more.
  • An electrolytic capacitor of 100 to 470 µF between 3V3 and GND close to the board acts like a small reservoir and covers the brief dips (the capacitor's negative leg goes to GND).
  • In an emergency you can lower the transmit power: WiFi.setTxPower(WIFI_POWER_8_5dBm); after WiFi.begin. The Wi-Fi range gets shorter, but the current drops too.
  • Never turn off the Brownout Detector in code (code you'll see on some forums). That's like pulling the fuse out of your house's fuse box: it hides the problem, and at low voltage the board starts behaving in strange and damaging ways.

Coach's advice

From this lesson on, every program that uses Wi-Fi has to pass one test: "Switch off the router in the middle." If your program froze, its buttons stopped responding, or it didn't come back on its own after the router was switched back on, that program is unfinished, even if it has worked fine on your desk for months. In a real home, the router will restart; it's not a question of "if" but "when". And one more rule: never write a while loop without a timeout for Wi-Fi. Every waiting loop you don't write is a bug you'll never have.

Lesson quiz

Your router has two networks: Home and Home_5G. The ESP32 scanner shows only Home. Why?

  1. The Home_5G network has a password
  2. The ESP32 supports only the 2.4 GHz band and doesn't see 5 GHz networks at all
  3. Home_5G is too far away
  4. The scanner shows only one network
The ESP32's radio is 802.11 b/g/n on 2.4 GHz only. Always connect it to the router's 2.4 GHz network.

Your board's IP is 192.168.1.37. A friend in another city types this address into their browser. What happens?

  1. Your board's page opens for them
  2. They reach your internet provider
  3. Your home router asks them for a password
  4. It never reaches your board; 192.168.x.x addresses are local and only mean something inside each home's own network
A local IP is like an apartment number: "apartment 37" exists in every building. From outside, only the public IP is visible.

Why did the STA_DISCONNECTED event function only set a variable to false, instead of calling WiFi.begin right there?

  1. Because WiFi.begin is allowed only once in the whole program
  2. Because it won't compile
  3. Because the event function runs in a separate task and must finish quickly; we leave the heavy work to loop and the millis watchdog
  4. Because setAutoReconnect does everything and the watchdog isn't needed
The event function is like a doorbell: it just announces. The main program makes the decisions and does the work.

Which time zone string is correct for Toronto, with automatic daylight saving time?

  1. "EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0"
  2. "EST-5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0"
  3. "EST5"
  4. "UTC0"
5 hours behind UTC is written as a positive 5 (the POSIX sign is reversed), and the rules after the comma tell the ESP32 when summer time starts and ends.

Your board's RSSI is -83 dBm and the connection keeps dropping. What is the best first step?

  1. Change the Wi-Fi password
  2. Move the board closer to the router or remove metal obstacles
  3. Set RETRY_MS to 1 second
  4. Lower WiFi.setTxPower
Below −80 is almost unusable. Fix the physical problem (distance, obstacles, antenna) first.

Full answer with explanation 
#include <WiFi.h>

const char* WIFI_SSID = "Wokwi-GUEST";    // put your own network name here
const char* WIFI_PASS = "";               // network password (empty for Wokwi)
const unsigned long TIMEOUT_MS = 15000;   // wait at most 15 seconds
const int LED_PIN = 4;                    // signal LED

// turns the status number into a readable name
const char* statusName(wl_status_t s) {
  switch (s) {
    case WL_IDLE_STATUS:     return "IDLE (0)";
    case WL_NO_SSID_AVAIL:   return "NO_SSID_AVAIL (1)";
    case WL_CONNECTED:       return "CONNECTED (3)";
    case WL_CONNECT_FAILED:  return "CONNECT_FAILED (4)";
    case WL_CONNECTION_LOST: return "CONNECTION_LOST (5)";
    case WL_DISCONNECTED:    return "DISCONNECTED (6)";
    default:                 return "OTHER";
  }
}

bool connectWiFi() {
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);
  Serial.printf("Connecting to %s", WIFI_SSID);

  unsigned long start = millis();
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) {
    if (millis() - start > TIMEOUT_MS) {            // timeout
      Serial.printf("\nFailed! status = %s\n", statusName(WiFi.status()));
      return false;
    }
    Serial.print(".");
    delay(500);
  }
  Serial.println("\nConnected!");
  return true;
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  if (connectWiFi()) {
    Serial.print("IP address : "); Serial.println(WiFi.localIP());
    Serial.print("Gateway    : "); Serial.println(WiFi.gatewayIP());
    Serial.print("MAC address: "); Serial.println(WiFi.macAddress());
    Serial.printf("RSSI       : %d dBm\n", WiFi.RSSI());
  } else {
    Serial.println("Check SSID, password, and that the network is 2.4 GHz");
  }
}

void loop() {
  static unsigned long lastBlink = 0, lastPrint = 0;
  static bool ledState = false;
  int rssi = WiFi.RSSI();                         // 0 when not connected
  bool connected = (WiFi.status() == WL_CONNECTED);
  unsigned long interval = 0;                     // 0 = steady on

  if (connected && rssi > -60) {
    ledState = true;                              // good signal: steady on
  } else {
    interval = (connected && rssi > -75) ? 500 : 100;
    if (millis() - lastBlink >= interval) {
      lastBlink = millis();
      ledState = !ledState;
    }
  }
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, ledState ? HIGH : LOW);

  if (millis() - lastPrint >= 5000) {             // a report every 5 seconds
    lastPrint = millis();
    Serial.printf("status=%s  RSSI=%d dBm  LED=%s\n", statusName(WiFi.status()), rssi,
                  interval == 0 ? "steady" : (interval == 500 ? "slow blink" : "fast blink"));
  }
}
  1. Change 1: the LED pin. The rest of the top of the program (includes, network name and password, statusName and connectWiFi) is exactly the same as WiFiConnect. In the course pin plan the status LED is always on GPIO4.
  2. Change 2: make the pin an output. One line in setup, before connecting. The rest of setup is untouched: connecting with a timeout and printing the IP.
  3. Memory, and reading things once. static means the value is kept between passes of loop. RSSI and the connection state are read once per pass. interval = 0 means "steady on". Every condition uses the same number, like "read the clock once" in lesson 3.1.
  4. Good signal: steady on. Only if we really are connected and RSSI is better than −60. While disconnected, RSSI is zero, and zero is "bigger" than −60; without the connected check, being disconnected would be mistaken for "excellent signal".
  5. Slow or fast blinking. Connected and between −60 and −75: 500 ms; worse than that, or disconnected: 100 ms. Then the same millis pattern. The disconnected state goes into the "fast" group too, so one look at the LED tells you something is seriously wrong.
  6. Writing to the pin. Once per pass. Decide in several branches, write in one place: the code is tidier and bugs are easier to find.
  7. A report every 5 seconds. The status, the RSSI and what the LED is doing right now. The ?: expression is used twice in a row: if 0, "steady"; otherwise if 500, "slow blink"; otherwise "fast blink". This line lets you compare the LED with the RSSI number and make sure the −60 and −75 limits work.

In Wokwi the board connects to Wokwi-GUEST. The virtual network's RSSI doesn't change, so you'll only see one of the three states; compare the RSSI number in the Serial Monitor with what the LED does. Try the real changes on a real board.

Switch the router off, wait about half a minute, switch it on and wait until the board gets an IP again; then do it once more. A sample output (the numbers and reasons will differ at your place):

[WiFi] Connecting in background...
[WiFi] Linked to router, waiting for IP...
[WiFi] Got IP: 192.168.1.37
[Time] 2026-09-23 14:05:12  RSSI -54 dBm
[WiFi] Outage #1 started
[WiFi] Disconnected, reason 200 (BEACON_TIMEOUT)
[WiFi] Disconnected, reason 201 (NO_AP_FOUND)
[WiFi] Still offline -> reconnect()
[WiFi] Disconnected, reason 201 (NO_AP_FOUND)
...
[WiFi] Linked to router, waiting for IP...
[WiFi] Got IP: 192.168.1.37
[WiFi] Back after 71 s (outages so far: 1)
...
[WiFi] Outage #2 started
...
[WiFi] Got IP: 192.168.1.37
[WiFi] Back after 64 s (outages so far: 2)
  • Outage #N started is printed exactly once for each time you switch the router off, even if dozens of Disconnected lines follow it.
  • After the very first IP (when the board powers up) there is no Back after.
  • The "Back after" number should roughly match your phone's stopwatch. It counts from the moment the ESP32 noticed the outage (a few seconds after you switched off) until the router was fully up again; most routers take 30 to 60 seconds to boot.
  • After switching off and on twice, the last line says outages so far: 2.
Full answer with explanation

The rest of the program is exactly RobustWiFi (with the same secrets.h file next to it). Three places changed:

#include <WiFi.h>
#include <time.h>
#include "secrets.h"                          // WIFI_SSID and WIFI_PASS live here

const int LED_PIN = 4;                        // network status LED
const unsigned long RETRY_MS = 10000;         // one manual retry every 10 seconds
const char* TZ_INFO = "EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0"; // Toronto/New York: put yours

volatile bool wifiUp = false;                 // do we have an IP right now?
volatile uint32_t outageCount = 0;            // how many times Wi-Fi dropped
volatile unsigned long downSince = 0;         // when the current outage started
volatile bool waitingForReturn = false;       // true during a counted outage
unsigned long lastRetry = 0, lastBlink = 0, lastPrint = 0;
bool ledState = false;

// the Wi-Fi library itself calls this function whenever something happens
void onWiFiEvent(WiFiEvent_t event, WiFiEventInfo_t info) {
  switch (event) {
    case ARDUINO_EVENT_WIFI_STA_CONNECTED:
      Serial.println("[WiFi] Linked to router, waiting for IP...");
      break;
    case ARDUINO_EVENT_WIFI_STA_GOT_IP:
      wifiUp = true;
      Serial.print("[WiFi] Got IP: ");
      Serial.println(IPAddress(info.got_ip.ip_info.ip.addr));
      if (waitingForReturn) {                 // back after a counted outage
        waitingForReturn = false;
        Serial.printf("[WiFi] Back after %lu s (outages so far: %lu)\n",
                      (millis() - downSince) / 1000, (unsigned long)outageCount);
      }
      break;
    case ARDUINO_EVENT_WIFI_STA_DISCONNECTED:
      if (wifiUp) {                           // only the first event of an outage counts
        outageCount++;
        downSince = millis();
        waitingForReturn = true;
        Serial.printf("[WiFi] Outage #%lu started\n", (unsigned long)outageCount);
      }
      wifiUp = false;
      Serial.printf("[WiFi] Disconnected, reason %d (%s)\n",
                    info.wifi_sta_disconnected.reason,
                    WiFi.disconnectReasonName((wifi_err_reason_t)info.wifi_sta_disconnected.reason));
      break;
    default:
      break;
  }
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);

  WiFi.onEvent(onWiFiEvent);                  // register before begin
  WiFi.setHostname("esp32-lab");              // the board's name in the router's list
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
  WiFi.setAutoReconnect(true);                // reconnect by itself after a dropout
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);           // we don't wait!
  Serial.println("[WiFi] Connecting in background...");

  configTzTime(TZ_INFO, "pool.ntp.org", "time.google.com");
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long now = millis();

  // 1) watchdog: if we're still offline, give a small nudge every 10 seconds
  if (!wifiUp && now - lastRetry >= RETRY_MS) {
    lastRetry = now;
    Serial.println("[WiFi] Still offline -> reconnect()");
    WiFi.disconnect();
    WiFi.reconnect();
  }

  // 2) LED: connected = steady on, disconnected = fast blink
  if (wifiUp) {
    ledState = true;
  } else if (now - lastBlink >= 200) {
    lastBlink = now;
    ledState = !ledState;
  }
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, ledState ? HIGH : LOW);

  // 3) print the time and signal strength every 5 seconds
  if (now - lastPrint >= 5000) {
    lastPrint = now;
    struct tm t;
    if (getLocalTime(&t, 10)) {               // wait at most 10 milliseconds
      char buf[24];
      strftime(buf, sizeof(buf), "%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S", &t);
      Serial.printf("[Time] %s  RSSI %d dBm\n", buf, WiFi.RSSI());
    } else {
      Serial.println("[Time] not synced yet");
    }
  }
}
  1. Three new variables. A counter of outages, the time the current outage started, and a flag that says "we are waiting for it to come back". They are changed in the event function, which the Wi-Fi library calls from another task; volatile tells the compiler to read the fresh value from memory every time. unsigned long is the same type as millis().
  2. In GOT_IP: "back after X seconds". If a counted outage was in progress, its length (milliseconds divided by 1000) and the total number of outages are printed. The flag is needed because the first GOT_IP after power-up is not a return from an outage and must not print "Back after".
  3. In DISCONNECTED: only the first time. If wifiUp is still true, this is the first event of the outage: the counter goes up by one, the time is stored and the flag is set. After that the existing wifiUp = false line runs. While the router is off, DISCONNECTED repeats every few seconds (and the watchdog's reconnect() causes new events too); without this check, one 50-second outage would be counted dozens of times.
Exercise 3: two roles at once (AP+STA)

Write a program that both connects to your home router and creates its own network called ESP32-Lab. Print both IPs. Join ESP32-Lab with your phone and see what IP you get.

Hint

WiFi.mode(WIFI_AP_STA), then WiFi.softAP(...) and WiFi.begin(...). The station IP comes from WiFi.localIP() and the access point IP from WiFi.softAPIP(). Note: in this mode the AP's channel has to match the router's channel, because the ESP32 has only one radio.

What should you see?

Wokwi doesn't support AP mode, so do this exercise with a real board and a phone. A sample output (the station IP and the channel come from your router):

Connecting to router.....
STA IP (from router): 192.168.1.37
Router channel: 6
AP started
AP IP (ESP32-Lab):   192.168.4.1
STA connected  |  AP clients: 0
STA connected  |  AP clients: 1
  • Two IPs from two different families: the station IP looks like the other devices at home (for example 192.168.1.x), and the access point IP is 192.168.4.1.
  • You see ESP32-Lab in the phone's Wi-Fi list. Join it with the password lab12345 and look at the connection details: the phone usually gets 192.168.4.2 (the first device) and its router or gateway is 192.168.4.1.
  • After the phone joins, the status line shows AP clients: 1 and the station is still connected.

If it doesn't work:

  • The phone says "no internet": that's correct! The ESP32 doesn't pass the router's internet on to its own network. Tap "stay connected".
  • AP failed: the password is shorter than 8 characters.
  • Router not reachable: the name or password in secrets.h is wrong, or the router is 5 GHz only. The AP still starts on channel 1, but because the station keeps searching for the router, it may be unstable.
Full answer with explanation 
#include <WiFi.h>
#include "secrets.h"                            // WIFI_SSID and WIFI_PASS of your home router

const char* AP_SSID = "ESP32-Lab";
const char* AP_PASS = "lab12345";               // at least 8 characters

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_AP_STA);                       // both roles at once

  // 1) station: connect to the home router first (at most 15 s)
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);
  Serial.print("Connecting to router");
  unsigned long start = millis();
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED && millis() - start < 15000) {
    Serial.print(".");
    delay(500);
  }
  Serial.println();
  int channel = 1;                              // used if the router can't be reached
  if (WiFi.status() == WL_CONNECTED) {
    channel = WiFi.channel();
    Serial.print("STA IP (from router): ");
    Serial.println(WiFi.localIP());
    Serial.printf("Router channel: %d\n", channel);
  } else {
    Serial.println("Router not reachable; starting the AP anyway");
  }

  // 2) access point: our own network, on the router's channel
  bool ok = WiFi.softAP(AP_SSID, AP_PASS, channel);
  Serial.println(ok ? "AP started" : "AP failed");
  Serial.print("AP IP (ESP32-Lab):   ");
  Serial.println(WiFi.softAPIP());
}

void loop() {
  static unsigned long last = 0;
  if (millis() - last >= 5000) {                // a status line every 5 seconds
    last = millis();
    Serial.printf("STA %s  |  AP clients: %d\n",
                  WiFi.status() == WL_CONNECTED ? "connected" : "offline",
                  WiFi.softAPgetStationNum());
  }
}
  1. Two networks, two passwords. The router password lives in secrets.h (as in RobustWiFi); the name and password of our own network are here. The AP password must be at least 8 characters, or softAP fails.
  2. Both roles. The ESP32 is a station (like a phone) and an access point (like a router) at the same time. In this mode you have two MAC addresses and two IPs, but only one radio.
  3. Station first. Connect with a 15-second timeout (as in WiFiConnect), then print the station IP and the router's channel. We need the router's channel first: the radio works on only one channel, so the AP has to use the router's channel. If we started the AP on channel 1 and the router was on channel 6, the AP would jump to channel 6 mid-way and the phone would drop for a moment.
  4. Then the access point. The ESP32-Lab network is created on the same channel and its IP is printed (always 192.168.4.1 unless you change it). The router hands out the station IP (for example 192.168.1.x), and the ESP32 itself hands out the AP's addresses; these are two separate networks.
  5. A status line. Every 5 seconds: whether the station is connected, and how many devices are on the AP. This line shows that the phone joining ESP32-Lab doesn't break the connection to the router.
The lesson in three lines

The ESP32 sees only 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, gets a local IP from the router through DHCP, and needs an RSSI better than −70 for stable operation. WiFi.begin only starts connecting; with onEvent (GOT_IP and DISCONNECTED), setAutoReconnect and a millis() watchdog you build a connection that never locks up loop. Keep your password in secrets.h and get local time with configTzTime("EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0", ...) (or your own city's string).
