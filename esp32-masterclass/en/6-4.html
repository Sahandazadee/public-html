A word from your coach, before we start

No course covers every part and every project. The difference between someone who "took a course" and an "engineer" isn't that the engineer knows everything in advance; it's that the engineer has a method. Faced with something unfamiliar, they don't panic, they don't copy-paste and pray it works. They go step by step and prove each step. This lesson gives you that method. Read it slowly.

1. From idea to device: a repeatable method

In the final project we ran through this method once together. Now we'll turn it into a general "recipe" that works for any project, from a simple night light to a robot.

Like building a house

No builder puts the roof on first. First the plan (requirements), then the materials (parts), then the foundation (power), then the walls one at a time, checking each with a level (testing each part), and finally paint and trim (the enclosure). If a crack shows up, the builder knows which wall it's in, because each was built separately. An electronics project is the same: if you connect everything at once and it doesn't work, you have no idea where the problem is.

Steps 1 and 2: the idea and the requirements (a small PRD)

"I want a smart plant pot" isn't a requirement; it's a wish. A requirement has to be measurable, so that at the end you can say "done". Copy this template and fill it in for every project. Half an hour of writing saves you dozens of hours of rework.

Project: ______________________ (one sentence: what problem does it solve?)
User: who uses it, where, and how many times a day?

Must do (Must):
  - M1: ... (measurable: "read the soil moisture every 10 minutes")
  - M2: ...
Nice to have (Nice):
  - N1: ...

Constraints:
  - Power: USB / battery ___ mAh / adapter ___ V
  - Target battery life: ___ days
  - Communication: Wi-Fi / BLE / ESP-NOW / none
  - Environment: indoors / outdoors / humidity / temperature ___
  - Size and budget: ___ × ___ mm, at most ___ (your currency)

Acceptance criteria (how do I know it's done?):
  - A1: ...
  - A2: ...

Out of scope (this version will NOT do):
  - ...
  1. One sentence and one user. If you can't describe the project in one sentence, you don't know what you want yet. The "user" decides how simple, tough or good-looking the device has to be. A device your grandmother uses needs a big button, not a serial menu.
  2. Must versus Nice. Only things without which the project is pointless go under Must. Beginners make everything a Must (a display, a phone app, Telegram alerts...) and never finish. Finish the Musts first; the Nices are the reward for version 2.
  3. Constraints. Power, battery life, type of communication, environment and budget. These lines choose the chip for you: "6 months on a battery" means deep sleep and a low-power board; "outdoors" means a waterproof box and maybe LoRa instead of Wi-Fi.
  4. Acceptance criteria. The tests you run at the end, such as "if the soil gets dry, the pump runs for at most 5 seconds". Without acceptance criteria, a project either never ends or is declared "done" too early and fails on its first night.
  5. Out of scope. Write down explicitly what you will not do. This line prevents "scope creep": the urge to add "just one more feature" that sets the project back by weeks.

Step 3: choose the sensors and actuators

For every Must, ask: "What do I have to measure to know this?" (a sensor) and "What do I have to move or switch on to do this?" (an actuator). Then ask three questions about each one:

  1. What voltage does it run on? 3.3 V, 5 V or 12 V? If its output is 5 V, don't connect it straight to an ESP32 pin (the pins aren't 5 V tolerant; lesson 1.3).
  2. What interface does it speak? Plain digital, analog, I2C, SPI, UART, or a special single-wire protocol (like the DHT22)? This decides how many pins you need, and which ones.
  3. How much current does it draw? Never power a motor, pump or relay straight from a pin; you need a transistor or MOSFET (lesson 3.4).

Step 4: the chip and the board

This is so important that section 2 is dedicated to it. For now, a simple rule: if you have no particular reason, a plain ESP32 (DevKitC or DOIT) is a safe choice, because every lesson in this course was written for it.

Step 6: the power budget

A power budget means answering two questions with numbers: (1) How much current must the power supply deliver at the worst moment? (2) How many days will the battery last?

Do the math: a battery-powered smart plant pot

Peak current: while transmitting over Wi-Fi, the ESP32 briefly draws up to about 250 mA, and a small 5 V pump about 200 mA. So the supply must deliver at least 250 + 200 ≈ 450mA without the voltage sagging; with a safety margin, a 1 A supply.

Average current: the device wakes every 10 minutes (600 seconds), works for 5 seconds at an average of 120 mA, and spends the remaining 595 seconds in deep sleep at 0.15 mA (on a low-power board; an ordinary DevKit draws several mA because of its regulator and USB chip):

I_avg = (5 × 120 + 595 × 0.15) ÷ 600 ≈ 1.15mA

Life of a 2000 mAh battery: 2000 ÷ 1.15 ≈ 1740h ≈ 72 days. With a 0.7 safety factor (the battery never fully empties, temperature, aging): about 50 days. The details of deep sleep are in lesson 6.1.

Step 7: breadboard, one at a time, each part with a test sketch

For each part, write a separate small sketch (or run its library example) that tests only that part. Don't throw these sketches away; keep them in a folder like tests/. Six months later, when the device breaks, they'll tell you in five minutes which part died. You'll find a ready-made template for this in section 4.

Step 8: integrating without delay

Once every part works on its own, add them to the main program one at a time, and test after each addition. Write the scheduling with millis() (lessons 3.1 and 6.3), or put slow jobs like networking in a separate task (lesson 5.4). A delay(5000) in the middle of the program means your button is deaf for 5 seconds.

Step 9: an enclosure or a PCB

A breadboard is great for learning and terrible for everyday use: wires work loose, moisture corrodes the contacts and vibration disconnects everything. Solder the final version onto perfboard (lesson 1.4) or design a real PCB. The KiCad 9 PCB design tutorial on this site (in Persian) is the natural next step.

Why so many steps for a simple project?

For a one-LED project, this method is overkill. But you build the habit on simple projects so that when you reach a project with ten parts, you do it automatically. The experience of workshops is simple: most projects that get abandoned die at the "I wired it all up at once and it didn't work" stage.

The ESP32 family is like cars from one factory

The plain ESP32 is an all-round family car. The S3 is a powerful SUV with a big trunk (PSRAM) for heavy loads like cameras and audio. The P4 is a powerful truck with no radio: image processing and big displays, but it needs a wireless companion to reach the internet.

Decision tree: which chip?

The golden rule of buying

Walk the decision tree first, then buy the board; not the other way round. And always buy two: one to build with, and one for when you fry the first with a wiring mistake, or want to find out whether the problem is the board or the code. A second board is the cheapest debugging tool in the world.

The trap: "newer = better"

The S3, C3 and C6 aren't "better ESP32s"; they're "different ESP32s". A project that used BluetoothSerial to connect to an Android Bluetooth terminal app won't compile on any of them. A project that played sound with dacWrite has no DAC on the S3, C3 or C6. Before switching chips, check the capability rows for everything your project needs.

3. Porting a sketch between models (hands-on workshop)

Say you have a project on a plain ESP32 and want to move it to a small C3 or an S3. The C++ code is the same, and most Arduino functions (digitalWrite, analogRead, WiFi, Wire...) work on all of them. But six things almost always break. One at a time:

No C3 or S3 board? Wokwi is the default path for this workshop

You don't need to buy anything to do this workshop. Wokwi simulates the ESP32-C3 and the ESP32-S3 as well as the plain ESP32, so the standard path is: (1) run the portable sketch below on the ESP32 in Wokwi; (2) open a new ESP32-C3 project in Wokwi, paste the same sketch and the C3 diagram.json further down, and press ▶; (3) do the same for the ESP32-S3; (4) compare the three capability reports. The ready-made diagrams are right after the sketch. Buy a real C3 or S3 only when a project needs one; everything you learn here carries over. (Two breakages, the empty Serial Monitor and the upload mode, only show up on real boards; read them anyway, because you'll meet them the day you plug one in.)

Breakage 1: pin numbers

On the ESP32, GPIO34 is an analog input; on the C3 there's no GPIO34 at all (the C3 only has GPIO0 to GPIO21). So the first job: open the pinout of the new board and write the pin table from scratch.

On the S3, touchRead behaves the opposite way

On a plain ESP32, touching the pin lowers the touchRead value (for example from 60 to 15). On the S2 and S3 the values are much larger (thousands) and rise when touched. So the condition if (touchRead(T) < 30) that worked on the ESP32 will never be true on the S3. Always print the raw value first and set the threshold from the real number.

Breakage 4: the built-in LED and the RGB LED

On DOIT boards, the blue LED is on GPIO2. The ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 has an addressable RGB LED (like a WS2812) instead: on GPIO48 on board revision v1.0 and on GPIO38 on v1.1. On the ESP32-C3-DevKitM-1 it's on GPIO8. This LED won't light with a plain digitalWrite, because it needs serial data. In core 3.x the ready-made function rgbLedWrite(pin, r, g, b) does this, and the constant RGB_BUILTIN is defined for boards that have such an LED. (The old name of this function was neopixelWrite; it still works but is marked deprecated.)

Read the S3 board revision off the board itself

When you select "ESP32S3 Dev Module", the Arduino core assumes the LED is on GPIO48. If your board is v1.1 and the LED doesn't light, write rgbLedWrite(38, ...) directly instead of using RGB_BUILTIN. Cheap clone boards sometimes change both; check the seller's pinout.

Breakage 5 (trap number 1): the Serial Monitor is empty!

This is the most common problem for people moving from the ESP32 to a C3 or S3. The program uploads, the LED blinks, but the Serial Monitor shows nothing. Why?

On a plain ESP32, the USB cable goes to a converter chip (CP2102 or CH340), and that chip goes to the UART0 pins (GPIO1 and GPIO3). Serial in Arduino is UART0; so everything just works.

But the C3 and S3 (and the C6 and H2) have a native USB unit built in, called USB-Serial-JTAG. Small boards like the C3 SuperMini or the XIAO wire the USB connector straight to the chip's USB pins, with no converter chip at all. Now the question is: which one is Serial in your program?

  • If Tools › USB CDC On Boot is set to Disabled (the default for "ESP32C3 Dev Module" and "ESP32S3 Dev Module"), Serial is UART0 (C3: GPIO21 and GPIO20; S3: GPIO43 and GPIO44). Your text goes out on those pins, which aren't connected to USB. Result: an empty Serial Monitor.
  • If it's Enabled, Serial is connected to the native USB (this is called USB CDC: the standard USB "virtual serial port" class), and the text shows up in the Serial Monitor.
Two mailboxes

Imagine your house has two mailboxes: one at the front door (USB) and one around the back (UART0). By default, the mail carrier (your program) leaves the letters in the back mailbox. You're waiting at the front door, thinking no mail came. "USB CDC On Boot: Enabled" means telling the mail carrier "leave the letters at the front door".

Boards with a separate converter chip are different: the official ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 and ESP32-C3-DevKitM-1 have a USB-to-UART converter chip on board. The S3-DevKitC-1 even has two USB connectors, labeled "UART" and "USB" on the board. If you plug the cable into "UART", it works like a plain ESP32 and CDC On Boot should stay Disabled. If you plug into "USB", it must be Enabled. The simple rule: cable in the connector that goes straight to the chip = CDC On Boot: Enabled.

Losing the first messages from setup?

With native USB, the computer takes a few hundred milliseconds to recognize the port. If you print on the first line of setup, that message may be lost. That's why the sketch below says: "wait up to 3 seconds for Serial to be ready, then carry on". The 3-second limit is essential; otherwise a device running on a battery with no computer attached would wait forever.

Why does the port "disappear" on native-USB boards?

On a plain ESP32, the USB converter chip is always powered, and the COM port never goes away, even if your program crashes. But with native USB, the ESP32 itself plays the role of the USB device. If your program crashes, goes into deep sleep, or uses the USB pins (for example GPIO19 and GPIO20 on the S3) for something else, the port disappears from the computer. The BOOT + RST combination takes the chip into the bootloader in ROM, which is always intact, and brings USB back up. After uploading, press RST once to run the new program.

The professional solution: one sketch for every chip

Instead of keeping three separate copies of the program, we use preprocessor directives (#if). Before compiling the code, the compiler keeps only the parts that match the chip you selected in the Board menu. There are two kinds of "flags":

  • CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32, CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32S3, CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32C3 and so on: these tell you which chip it is. They're good for pin numbers.
  • SOC capability macros such as SOC_DAC_SUPPORTED, SOC_TOUCH_SENSOR_SUPPORTED and SOC_BT_CLASSIC_SUPPORTED: these tell you what the chip has. They're better, because if a new chip with a DAC comes out tomorrow, your code works correctly without any changes.

The circuit for the ESP32 DevKitC version (on the S3 and C3 only the pin numbers change, as in the code):

// PortableDemo.ino — one sketch, three chips: ESP32, ESP32-S3, ESP32-C3
#include <Arduino.h>
#include "soc/soc_caps.h"   // SOC_… macros (each chip's hardware capabilities)

// ---------- 1) Pins: only this part differs per chip ----------
#if CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32
  const int LED_PIN    = 4;    // PWM output
  const int BUTTON_PIN = 27;
  const int POT_PIN    = 34;   // ADC1_CH6
  const int TOUCH_PIN  = 32;   // T9
  const int DAC_PIN    = 25;   // DAC1
#elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32S3
  const int LED_PIN    = 5;
  const int BUTTON_PIN = 4;
  const int POT_PIN    = 1;    // ADC1_CH0
  const int TOUCH_PIN  = 7;    // T7
#elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32C3
  const int LED_PIN    = 6;
  const int BUTTON_PIN = 7;
  const int POT_PIN    = 3;    // ADC1_CH3
#else
  #error "This sketch supports ESP32, ESP32-S3 and ESP32-C3 only"
#endif

// ---------- 2) Capability report ----------
void printChipInfo() {
  Serial.printf("Chip: %s rev %d, %d core(s) @ %lu MHz\n",
                ESP.getChipModel(), ESP.getChipRevision(),
                ESP.getChipCores(), (unsigned long)ESP.getCpuFreqMHz());
  Serial.printf("Flash: %lu KB, PSRAM: %lu KB\n",
                (unsigned long)(ESP.getFlashChipSize() / 1024),
                (unsigned long)(ESP.getPsramSize() / 1024));
#if SOC_DAC_SUPPORTED
  Serial.println("DAC: yes");
#else
  Serial.println("DAC: no  -> PWM only");
#endif
#if SOC_TOUCH_SENSOR_SUPPORTED
  Serial.println("Touch: yes");
#else
  Serial.println("Touch: no");
#endif
#if SOC_BT_CLASSIC_SUPPORTED
  Serial.println("Bluetooth Classic: yes (BluetoothSerial OK)");
#else
  Serial.println("Bluetooth Classic: no  -> use BLE");
#endif
#if ARDUINO_USB_CDC_ON_BOOT
  Serial.println("Serial = native USB (CDC)");
#else
  Serial.println("Serial = UART0");
#endif
}

// ---------- 3) setup ----------
void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  unsigned long t0 = millis();
  while (!Serial && millis() - t0 < 3000) delay(10);  // wait for USB, at most 3 seconds

  pinMode(BUTTON_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP);
  ledcAttach(LED_PIN, 5000, 8);        // core 3.x: pin, frequency, resolution
  printChipInfo();
}

// ---------- 4) loop ----------
void loop() {
  int raw  = analogRead(POT_PIN);      // 0..4095
  int duty = raw >> 4;                 // 0..255
  bool pressed = (digitalRead(BUTTON_PIN) == LOW);
  ledcWrite(LED_PIN, duty);

#if SOC_DAC_SUPPORTED
  dacWrite(DAC_PIN, duty);             // a real analog voltage, 0 to 3.3V
#endif

  Serial.printf("pot=%4d  button=%s", raw, pressed ? "DOWN" : "up  ");
#if SOC_TOUCH_SENSOR_SUPPORTED
  Serial.printf("  touch=%lu", (unsigned long)touchRead(TOUCH_PIN));
#endif
#ifdef RGB_BUILTIN
  if (pressed) rgbLedWrite(RGB_BUILTIN, 40, 0, 0);  // red
  else         rgbLedWrite(RGB_BUILTIN, 0, 40, 0);  // green
#endif
  Serial.println();
  delay(200);
}
  1. Including soc_caps.h. This file from ESP-IDF defines each chip's list of capabilities as macros. A dangerous trap: in C++, if you use a name in #if that isn't defined, you get no error; the compiler silently treats it as zero. If this file isn't included, #if SOC_DAC_SUPPORTED comes out as "no" even on the ESP32, and you'll never figure out why the DAC doesn't work. Including it explicitly closes this trap.
  2. Pins for the plain ESP32. If "ESP32 Dev Module" is selected in the Board menu, only these five lines are compiled. Every pin was chosen by the rules from lesson 1.3: GPIO34 is on ADC1 (no problem with Wi-Fi), GPIO32 is touch pin T9, and GPIO25 is the DAC1 output. None of them is a strapping pin.
  3. Pins for the S3. GPIO1 is on ADC1_CH0 and GPIO7 is touch pin T7. DAC_PIN isn't defined, because the S3 has no DAC. We avoided GPIO3, 45 and 46 (strapping), 19 and 20 (USB), and 26 to 37 (flash and PSRAM). Pins 1 to 18 are the safest choices on the S3.
  4. Pins for the C3. GPIO3 is on ADC1_CH3. No touch pin or DAC is defined, because the C3 has neither. We avoided GPIO2, 8 and 9 (strapping), 12 to 17 (flash), and 18 and 19 (USB). GPIO6 and GPIO7 are also external JTAG pins on the C3, but as long as you don't use external JTAG, they're ordinary GPIOs.
  5. #error for an unknown chip. If someone compiles this code for a C6, compilation stops with a clear message. A compile error is better than a program that compiles and then runs on the board with the wrong pins. "Fail early and loudly" is an engineering principle.
  6. Printing the chip's specs. ESP.getChipModel() gives the chip name, getChipCores() the number of cores, getFlashChipSize() the flash size and getPsramSize() the PSRAM size. When you buy an unknown board online, these lines tell you in one second what you actually bought. If PSRAM is zero but the seller promised 8 megabytes, either the board is fake or you haven't enabled the Tools › PSRAM option.
  7. Capability macros. Each #if SOC_... block is compiled only if the chip has that capability. These lines both report to you and show the main pattern: always put feature-specific code inside that feature's macro, not inside a chip name.
  8. Where does Serial go? The Arduino IDE itself creates the ARDUINO_USB_CDC_ON_BOOT macro from the "USB CDC On Boot" option. If this line printed "UART0" and you saw it in the Serial Monitor, your cable is connected to a converter chip. If you saw nothing at all, go back to trap number 1.
  9. A limited wait for Serial. While Serial isn't ready and less than 3 seconds have passed, wait 10 ms at a time. On native USB, !Serial means "no computer has opened the port yet". On a plain ESP32 this condition passes almost immediately, so it does no harm. The time limit keeps a device with no computer from getting stuck.
  10. Button and PWM. INPUT_PULLUP for the button and ledcAttach(pin, 5000, 8) for PWM at 5 kHz with 8 bits (0 to 255). LEDC exists on all of these chips, so PWM is the most portable way to get a "pseudo-analog output". This is the core 3.x syntax; on 2.x you had to write ledcSetup and ledcAttachPin.
  11. Shared reading and writing. analogRead is 12-bit on all three chips (0 to 4095). raw >> 4 means dividing by 16 to get down to the 0 to 255 range. These lines have no #if at all, because they're the same on every chip. That's the goal: keep most of the code shared and fence off only the differences.
  12. DAC only where it exists. On the ESP32, the voltage on GPIO25 follows the potentiometer from 0 to about 3.3 V. On the C3 and S3 this line isn't compiled at all, so you don't get a "dacWrite was not declared" error either.
  13. Touch only where it exists. On the ESP32 and S3, the touch value is printed. touchRead returns a 16-bit type on the ESP32 and a 32-bit type on the S3. Casting to unsigned long makes a single printf correct for both.
  14. The built-in RGB LED, if there is one. #ifdef RGB_BUILTIN compiles only on boards whose board definition file (variant) declares this LED. Here we used #ifdef (is it defined?) instead of #if, because RGB_BUILTIN is a pin number, not a 0-or-1 flag.

4. Learning any part you've never seen (the core skill)

Tomorrow you'll reach for a part that wasn't in this course: a gas sensor, a motor driver, an e-ink display. No lesson is waiting for you. This five-step path always works:

Step 1: read the datasheet (without fear)

The BME280 datasheet is sixty pages long. You don't need to read all of it. It's like a restaurant menu: you look for a few specific sections. This "guided tour" works for any part; our example is Bosch's BME280 temperature, humidity and pressure sensor:

Steps 2 and 3: minimal wiring and a "check-up"

Only four wires: power, ground and two data wires. Before installing any library, prove with a small sketch that the part is alive and is what you think it is. You can use this template for any I2C part; just change the addresses, the ID register and the expected value from the datasheet.

// PartBringUp.ino — bring-up template for any new I2C part (example: BME280)
#include <Wire.h>

const int SDA_PIN = 21;          // ESP32 DevKitC; change on S3/C3
const int SCL_PIN = 22;
const uint8_t ID_REG      = 0xD0;  // from the datasheet: chip ID register
const uint8_t EXPECTED_ID = 0x60;  // BME280 = 0x60 , BMP280 = 0x58

uint8_t partAddr = 0;            // the address the scanner finds

// Step 1: search the whole bus
int scanBus() {
  int found = 0;
  for (uint8_t a = 1; a < 127; a++) {
    Wire.beginTransmission(a);
    if (Wire.endTransmission() == 0) {
      Serial.printf("  device at 0x%02X\n", a);
      if (a == 0x76 || a == 0x77) partAddr = a;   // possible BME280 addresses
      found++;
    }
  }
  return found;
}

// Step 2: read one byte from a register
bool readReg(uint8_t addr, uint8_t reg, uint8_t &val) {
  Wire.beginTransmission(addr);
  Wire.write(reg);
  if (Wire.endTransmission(false) != 0) return false;   // false = Repeated Start
  if (Wire.requestFrom(addr, (uint8_t)1) != 1) return false;
  val = Wire.read();
  return true;
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(1500);
  Wire.begin(SDA_PIN, SCL_PIN);
  Serial.println("\n[1] I2C scan:");
  if (scanBus() == 0) {
    Serial.println("  nothing found -> check VCC, GND, SDA/SCL swap, pull-ups");
    return;
  }
  if (partAddr == 0) { Serial.println("  part not at 0x76/0x77"); return; }

  uint8_t id;
  Serial.printf("[2] Reading ID register 0x%02X at 0x%02X...\n", ID_REG, partAddr);
  if (!readReg(partAddr, ID_REG, id)) { Serial.println("  read failed"); return; }
  Serial.printf("  ID = 0x%02X (expected 0x%02X) -> %s\n", id, EXPECTED_ID,
                id == EXPECTED_ID ? "OK, this is the right chip" : "DIFFERENT chip!");
  Serial.println("[3] Now install the library and run its example.");
}

void loop() {
  // empty on purpose: this program does a one-time "check-up"
}
  1. Everything from the datasheet at the top of the program. The pins, the ID register address and the value you expect. For the next part (say, a BH1750 light sensor or an MPU6050 accelerometer) you only change these four lines. A good template keeps everything that varies in one place.
  2. The I2C scanner. It says "hello" to every address from 1 to 126. If a part answers (ACK), endTransmission() returns zero and the address is printed. This separates the question "are the wiring and power right?" from "is the library right?". If the scanner finds nothing, no library can help you; it's a hardware problem.
  3. Reading a register. First it writes the register number, then, without releasing the bus (endTransmission(false) means a Repeated Start), it requests one byte. Almost every I2C part in the world uses this same "write the register number, then read" pattern. Once you understand this function, you can test any datasheet directly.
  4. Wire.begin with explicit pins. Wire.begin(SDA_PIN, SCL_PIN). The default I2C pins differ on each chip (ESP32: 21 and 22; S3 and C3: 8 and 9). Writing the pins out means that when you switch boards, you only change lines 4 and 5.
  5. Fail early, with a clear message. If nothing is found, it tells you exactly what to check, and stops. A bare "nothing found" message is useless; "check VCC, GND, an SDA/SCL swap, pull-ups" is a checklist. Give your future self a checklist, not a riddle.
  6. Comparing the ID with the datasheet. 0x60 means you have a genuine BME280; 0x58 means you bought a BMP280 (no humidity). These two lines save dozens of hours of confusion: "Why does the library give NaN for humidity?" Answer: because your sensor has no humidity sensor at all.
  7. An empty loop. This program does its check-up just once. To run the check-up again, press the EN (reset) button. Keeping a test sketch simple means that if something fails, you know it's the part and not complicated program logic.
[1] I2C scan: device at 0x76 [2] Reading ID register 0xD0 at 0x76... ID = 0x58 (expected 0x60) -> DIFFERENT chip! [3] Now install the library and run its example.

In the output above, the seller sent a BMP280 instead of a BME280. Now you know you need to install a BMP280 library, and you won't get humidity.

Step 4: find and evaluate a library

  1. Search in the Library Manager. In the Arduino IDE, open the library (books) icon in the sidebar (Sketch › Include Library › Manage Libraries...) and type the chip's name, for example BME280. For this part, Adafruit BME280 Library by Adafruit is a good choice. During installation it asks whether to also install the dependencies (Adafruit Unified Sensor and Adafruit BusIO); click Install All.
  2. Look at its GitHub repository. Follow the "More info" link to GitHub and check four things: the date of the last commit (less than a year or two ago?), the number of stars, the open issues (has anyone said it doesn't work on the ESP32?) and the examples folder.
  3. When choosing between several libraries, prefer: the module maker's library (Adafruit, SparkFun, DFRobot), or a library recommended in the chip's official documentation, or a library that explicitly supports the ESP32.
Signs of a risky library

The last change was 6 years ago; it uses removed functions like ledcSetup (which no longer exists in core 3.x); unanswered issues titled "does not compile on ESP32"; no examples folder. These mean you should either look for another library or be ready to fix it yourself.

Step 5: run the library example unchanged first, then change things "one at a time"

At the bottom of the File › Examples list, find the library's name (for example Adafruit BME280 Library › bme280test). Change only the pins or the address if needed, and run it. If the example works, you now have a "safe point". From here on, follow the one-change rule:

The one-change rule, like cooking a new recipe

If you change the salt, the cooking time and the type of oil all at once and the food turns out badly, you don't know which one was to blame. If you change only one thing each time and taste, you always know. In code: one change, compile, upload, test. If it breaks, undo just that last change.

  2. The wiring as text. Not "I wired it like in the photo". Most problems are found on this very line; sometimes you spot the mistake yourself while writing it.
  3. Observations, evidence and what you've already tried. It shows you've made an effort, and the person answering won't waste time suggesting things you've already tried.
  4. Minimal code. The shortest complete program that shows the problem, not a thousand-line project. In 50% of cases, building this short code finds the problem by itself. That's a debugging technique in its own right.

5. The troubleshooting handbook

Troubleshooting isn't guessing. It's a process: look at the symptom → list the likely causes → rule one in or out with a simple test. This map covers the most common symptoms:

Endless resets: read the reset reason first

Every time the ESP32 resets, it stores the reason in a register. This "black box" is like an airplane's: after a crash, the first job is to read it. This sketch prints the reset reason, shows the different log levels, and lets you trigger a crash on purpose so you can practice reading a Guru Meditation.

// DebugStart.ino — reset reason + leveled logging + a deliberate crash for practice
#include <Arduino.h>
#include "esp_system.h"

RTC_NOINIT_ATTR uint32_t crashCount;   // not cleared by a software reset

const char *resetReasonText(esp_reset_reason_t r) {
  switch (r) {
    case ESP_RST_POWERON:   return "POWERON  (power was applied)";
    case ESP_RST_EXT:       return "EXT      (external reset pin)";
    case ESP_RST_SW:        return "SW       (ESP.restart() was called)";
    case ESP_RST_PANIC:     return "PANIC    (crash / Guru Meditation)";
    case ESP_RST_INT_WDT:   return "INT_WDT  (interrupt watchdog)";
    case ESP_RST_TASK_WDT:  return "TASK_WDT (a task blocked too long)";
    case ESP_RST_WDT:       return "WDT      (other watchdog)";
    case ESP_RST_DEEPSLEEP: return "DEEPSLEEP (woke up from deep sleep)";
    case ESP_RST_BROWNOUT:  return "BROWNOUT (supply voltage dropped!)";
    default:                return "OTHER/UNKNOWN";
  }
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(1500);                          // time for the Serial Monitor to open
  Serial.setDebugOutput(true);          // send log_x messages to this Serial too

  esp_reset_reason_t why = esp_reset_reason();
  if (why == ESP_RST_POWERON) crashCount = 0;   // after a power cut its value is random
  if (why == ESP_RST_PANIC || why == ESP_RST_TASK_WDT ||
      why == ESP_RST_INT_WDT || why == ESP_RST_BROWNOUT) crashCount++;

  Serial.printf("\n=== Boot. Reset reason: %s\n", resetReasonText(why));
  Serial.printf("=== Abnormal resets since power-on: %lu\n", (unsigned long)crashCount);
  Serial.printf("=== Chip: %s, free heap: %lu bytes\n",
                ESP.getChipModel(), (unsigned long)ESP.getFreeHeap());

  log_e("ERROR level: shown if Core Debug Level >= Error");
  log_w("WARN  level: shown if Core Debug Level >= Warn");
  log_i("INFO  level: shown if Core Debug Level >= Info");
  log_d("DEBUG level: shown if Core Debug Level >= Debug");
  log_v("VERBOSE level: shown only at Verbose");

  Serial.println("Send 'c' = crash, 'r' = restart, 'h' = heap");
}

void loop() {
  static unsigned long last = 0;
  if (millis() - last >= 5000) {        // a "heartbeat" every 5 seconds
    last = millis();
    uint32_t heap = ESP.getFreeHeap();
    log_i("alive, uptime=%lus heap=%lu", millis() / 1000, (unsigned long)heap);
    if (heap < 20000) log_w("heap is getting low!");
  }

  if (Serial.available()) {
    char cmd = Serial.read();
    if (cmd == 'c') {
      Serial.println("Crashing on purpose (null pointer write)...");
      Serial.flush();
      volatile int *p = nullptr;
      *p = 42;                          // write to address zero → Guru Meditation
    } else if (cmd == 'r') {
      ESP.restart();
    } else if (cmd == 'h') {
      Serial.printf("heap now=%lu, lowest ever=%lu\n",
                    (unsigned long)ESP.getFreeHeap(),
                    (unsigned long)ESP.getMinFreeHeap());
    }
  }
}
  1. A variable that survives a software reset. RTC_NOINIT_ATTR puts the variable in RTC memory and doesn't zero it at boot. An ordinary variable is zeroed on every reset, so you couldn't count crashes with it. This variable keeps its value after a crash or ESP.restart(); only after a power cut does it hold a random value, which is why we zero it on line 28.
  2. Turning the reset code into text. esp_reset_reason() returns one of the ESP_RST_... values. The most important ones: PANIC means the program crashed; BROWNOUT means the voltage dropped (a hardware problem!); TASK_WDT means a job took too long; POWERON means power was just connected. That single word settles half of the troubleshooting.
  3. Serial and setDebugOutput. Serial.setDebugOutput(true) also sends the internal logs and log_x messages to this same Serial. On a plain ESP32 the logs go to UART0 anyway. On a C3 or S3 with CDC On Boot enabled, without this line the logs may go to UART0 and you won't see them in the Serial Monitor.
  4. Counting abnormal resets. Zero after power-up, and one more with every crash, watchdog or brownout. This number gives away a wall-mounted device that "sometimes" resets. You can also send it over MQTT (lesson 5.1) to see it remotely.
  5. Five log levels. log_e (error), log_w (warning), log_i (info), log_d (debug) and log_v (verbose). These are printed only if Tools › Core Debug Level is set to that level or higher. The default is None, so none of them print. The big advantage: in the final version you set the level to None and these lines aren't compiled at all; they take neither space nor time. You can't do that with Serial.println. Each log line also shows the file name, line number and function name.
  6. The heartbeat. Every 5 seconds it logs the uptime and the free memory (heap), without delay. If the heap slowly shrinks, you have a "memory leak" (for example a big String that keeps getting built). A device that crashes after 3 days usually has exactly this problem.
  7. A deliberate crash. Sending the letter c from the Serial Monitor makes it write to address zero (a null pointer). The best time to learn to read a Guru Meditation is when you already know the cause. volatile stops the compiler from removing this "silly" line. Serial.flush() makes sure the message is fully sent before the crash.
  8. Software reset and memory report. r does a clean reset (the reset reason is then SW); h shows the current free memory and the lowest free memory ever. getMinFreeHeap() shows the worst moment; if it has dropped to a few kilobytes, your device is on the edge of a cliff, even if it looks fine right now.

After sending c on a plain ESP32 you'll see something like this (with Core Debug Level set to Info):

Crashing on purpose (null pointer write)... Guru Meditation Error: Core 1 panic'ed (StoreProhibited). Exception was unhandled. Core 1 register dump: PC : 0x400d1f2e PS : 0x00060830 A0 : 0x800d4b10 A1 : 0x3ffb2260 ... EXCVADDR: 0x00000000 LBEG : 0x4008a5e1 LEND : 0x4008a5f1 LCOUNT : 0x00000000 Backtrace: 0x400d1f2b:0x3ffb2260 0x400d4b0d:0x3ffb2290 0x4008d1e2:0x3ffb22b0 Rebooting... ... === Boot. Reset reason: PANIC (crash / Guru Meditation) === Abnormal resets since power-on: 1

Here's how to read this text:

  • Guru Meditation Error: Espressif's playful name for "the program crashed" (borrowed from the error message of old Amiga computers).
  • StoreProhibited: the type of error. It means "writing to an address you're not allowed to write to". EXCVADDR: 0x00000000 says that address was zero: a null pointer. LoadProhibited means reading from a forbidden address. On RISC-V chips (C3, C6, H2) the names are different: Store access fault and Load access fault.
  • Backtrace: the list of addresses of the functions that had been called, in order; like footprints in the snow. These addresses mean nothing to a human, so they have to be "translated".

Translating the Backtrace with ESP Exception Decoder

Arduino IDE 1.x had a tool called EspExceptionDecoder. For Arduino IDE 2.x its replacement is the esp-exception-decoder extension by dankeboy36:

  1. Download. From that GitHub repository's Releases page, get the latest .vsix file from the 1.x line. According to the project's own README, the 2.x versions are built for VS Code and won't run in the Arduino IDE (they may even stop the IDE from starting).

On RISC-V chips, the crash output has a long "Stack memory" dump instead of a simple one-line Backtrace; copy all of it.

Brownout, and "works on USB but not on the battery"

The message Brownout detector was triggered means the chip's supply voltage briefly dropped below the allowed limit and the ESP32 reset itself for protection. It almost always happens when Wi-Fi turns on, because at that moment the current suddenly jumps to about 250 mA.

  • A long, thin USB cable: the resistance of the wires drops the voltage under load. Try a short, thick cable.
  • A weak USB port, or a hub without its own power adapter.
  • A battery with an unsuitable regulator: the AMS1117 regulator found on many boards drops about 1 volt. A 3.7 V lithium cell that has fallen to 3.6 V gives only about 2.6 V after the regulator: less than needed. (Lessons 6.1 and 1.4)
  • A temporary helper fix: a 100 to 470 µF electrolytic capacitor between 3V3 and GND, close to the board, smooths out short spikes.
Don't turn off the brownout detector

Online you'll find code that disables the brownout detector by writing to a register. That's like pulling the battery out of a smoke alarm because it keeps going off. A chip running on too little voltage can behave strangely or even write garbage to the flash. Fix the power problem, not the message.

Measuring tools

Multimeter (lesson 1.4): three measurements that find 80% of hardware problems: (1) the 3V3 voltage relative to GND while Wi-Fi is on (it should stay above 3.1 V); (2) the voltage on the sensor's power pin, measured at the sensor itself, not at the board; (3) continuity (the beep) of every suspicious wire.

Logic analyzer: when the question is "is anything going over the I2C, SPI or UART wire at all?", a multimeter can't help, because the signal changes millions of times a second. A cheap "24MHz 8-channel" logic analyzer (clones based on the CY7C68013A chip) together with the free PulseView software records the signals and even decodes the protocol: "the ESP32 wrote to address 0x76, the sensor didn't ACK". The sampling rule: at least 4 times the signal speed; for I2C at 400 kHz, that means sampling at 2 to 4 MHz or more. This tool's inputs are for 3.3 V and 5 V logic levels; never connect it to a higher voltage, and always connect its GND to the circuit's GND.

Multimeter versus logic analyzer

A multimeter is like taking a photo of a street: it tells you what's happening "right now". A logic analyzer is like a slow-motion video camera: it records every car that passes, with its exact time. For the high-speed traffic of protocols, you need the video.

Lesson quiz

On an ESP32-C3 SuperMini (no USB converter chip) the program uploads, but the Serial Monitor shows nothing. What's the most likely cause?

  1. The Serial Monitor speed is wrong
  2. The C3 has no Serial at all
  3. The USB CDC On Boot option is off, so Serial goes to UART0, not USB
  4. The CP2102 driver isn't installed
On native-USB boards, Serial is connected to USB only when USB CDC On Boot is Enabled.

Your project needs a camera, Wi-Fi and lots of memory for image processing. Which is the best choice?

  1. ESP32-C3
  2. ESP32-S3 with PSRAM
  3. ESP32-H2
  4. ESP32-C6
The S3 has a camera interface (LCD_CAM), PSRAM and two 240 MHz cores. The P4 is more powerful but has no built-in Wi-Fi.

Why is #if SOC_DAC_SUPPORTED better than #if CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32 for guarding dacWrite?

  1. Because the program runs faster
  2. Because CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32 isn't defined on the ESP32
  3. Because SOC_ is checked at run time
  4. Because it asks about the "capability"; if another chip (like the S2) also has a DAC, it automatically works correctly
The ESP32-S2 also has a DAC. With the capability macro, your code uses the DAC on the S2 too, without any changes.

In Serial you see this line: Guru Meditation Error: Core 1 panic'ed (LoadProhibited) and EXCVADDR: 0x00000000. What does it mean?

  1. The program read from a null pointer (address zero)
  2. The supply voltage dropped
  3. An endless loop triggered the watchdog
  4. The compiled file is corrupt
Load means reading, and EXCVADDR of zero means the faulting address was zero: a pointer or object that was never initialized. Find the exact line with the Exception Decoder.

A module you bought as a "BME280" shows up on 0x76 in the scanner, but the library gives NaN for humidity. What's the logical next step?

  1. Install a different library
  2. Change the address to 0x77
  3. Read the chip ID register (0xD0) to see whether it's 0x60 or 0x58
  4. Swap the wires
0x58 means a BMP280, which has no humidity sensor at all. The datasheet + one register settles the question for certain.

On an ESP32-C3 you've connected a button with an external pull-down to GPIO9. What happens?

  1. Nothing; GPIO9 is an ordinary pin
  2. At power-up GPIO9 is LOW and the board goes into download mode; the program doesn't run
  3. The flash gets damaged
  4. USB disconnects
GPIO9 is the C3's boot pin (the BOOT button itself). If it's LOW at reset, that means download mode. Move the button to another pin, or wire it with a pull-up as active-LOW and don't press it while powering up.

Hint

What should you see?

There's no single right PRD, so grade your own with these criteria. You pass if all of them are true:

  • Every Must is measurable (a number, a time or a yes/no test), not a wish like "waters the plant well".
  • At least two acceptance criteria, each one a test you can actually run.
  • Every sensor and actuator has its voltage, its interface and its current written next to it.
  • No pin without a reason; no analog input on an ADC2 pin (0, 2, 4, 12 to 15, 25 to 27) when the project uses Wi-Fi; no heavy load straight on a pin.
  • Peak current and average current as numbers, and a power supply chosen from the peak.

Sanity check for the numbers: with a small pump, the peak lands around 400 to 500 mA (Wi-Fi about 250 mA plus the pump), so a 1 A supply. An always-on ESP32 with Wi-Fi averages tens of milliamps, which empties a 2000 mAh battery in about a day. If your battery project promises weeks without deep sleep, the numbers are wrong.

#include <Arduino.h>
#include "soc/soc_caps.h"   // SOC_… macros (each chip's hardware capabilities)

// ---------- 1) Pins: only this part differs per chip ----------
#if CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32
  const int LED_PIN    = 4;    // PWM output
  const int BUTTON_PIN = 27;
  const int POT_PIN    = 34;   // ADC1_CH6
  const int TOUCH_PIN  = 32;   // T9
  const int DAC_PIN    = 25;   // DAC1
#elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32S3

  const int BUTTON_PIN = 4;
  const int POT_PIN    = 1;    // ADC1_CH0
  const int TOUCH_PIN  = 7;    // T7
#elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32C3

  const int BUTTON_PIN = 7;
  const int POT_PIN    = 3;    // ADC1_CH3
#elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32C6
  const int LED_PIN    = 18;
  const int BUTTON_PIN = 19;
  const int POT_PIN    = 2;    // ADC1_CH2
#else

// ---------- 2) Capability report ----------

  Serial.printf("Flash: %lu KB, PSRAM: %lu KB\n",
                (unsigned long)(ESP.getFlashChipSize() / 1024),
                (unsigned long)(ESP.getPsramSize() / 1024));
#if SOC_DAC_SUPPORTED
  Serial.println("DAC: yes");
#else
  Serial.println("DAC: no  -> PWM only");
#endif
#if SOC_TOUCH_SENSOR_SUPPORTED
  Serial.println("Touch: yes");
#else
  Serial.println("Touch: no");
#endif
#if SOC_BT_CLASSIC_SUPPORTED
  Serial.println("Bluetooth Classic: yes (BluetoothSerial OK)");
#else
  Serial.println("Bluetooth Classic: no  -> use BLE");
#endif
#if ARDUINO_USB_CDC_ON_BOOT
  Serial.println("Serial = native USB (CDC)");
#else

#endif

// ---------- 3) setup ----------
void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  unsigned long t0 = millis();
  while (!Serial && millis() - t0 < 3000) delay(10);  // wait for USB, at most 3 seconds

  pinMode(BUTTON_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP);
  ledcAttach(LED_PIN, 5000, 8);        // core 3.x: pin, frequency, resolution
  printChipInfo();

// ---------- 4) loop ----------

  int raw  = analogRead(POT_PIN);      // 0..4095
  int duty = raw >> 4;                 // 0..255

  dacWrite(DAC_PIN, duty);             // a real analog voltage, 0 to 3.3V
#endif

Full answer with explanation
  1. (a) The crash line: 61. The Decoder shows loop() at DebugStart.ino:61, the line *p = 42;. The first line of the translated Backtrace is where the crash happened; the lines below it are the functions that called it (here the core's loop task). StoreProhibited with EXCVADDR: 0x00000000 confirms it: a write to address zero.
  2. (b) The counter after the crash: 1, reason PANIC. On the fresh power-up, line 28 set the counter to 0. The crash caused a PANIC reset; PANIC is in the condition on lines 29 and 30, so the counter went from 0 to 1.
  3. (c) After r: reason SW, counter still 1. ESP.restart() is a planned software reset, and SW isn't in the list on lines 29 and 30 (PANIC, TASK_WDT, INT_WDT, BROWNOUT), so nothing is added. It isn't reset to 0 either, because line 28 only does that on POWERON.

Grading: all three numbers right (61; 1 with PANIC; SW with 1) and, for (c), the reason: "SW isn't counted as abnormal".

Practice project: port the station to the ESP32-C3

Your station from lesson 6.3 works on the ESP32. Now someone wants a smaller, cheaper version on an ESP32-C3. Copying the sketch and editing pin numbers by hand would leave you with two programs to maintain forever. Instead you'll turn it into one sketch that compiles for both chips, choosing pins per chip and features per capability, exactly like PortableDemo in the porting workshop.

Requirements

  1. One source file. The pin numbers live only in one #if CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32 / #elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32C3 block; any other chip stops the build with #error.
  2. The C3 pins follow the C3's rules: the LDR on an ADC1 pin (GPIO0 to 4), nothing on the strapping pins (2, 8, 9), the flash pins (12 to 17) or the USB pins (18, 19), and the I2C pins passed to Wire.begin(SDA, SCL) explicitly.
  3. setup() waits at most 3 seconds for the Serial port (native USB), then prints a short report: chip model, number of cores, the pins in use, and "Chip temperature sensor: yes/no" decided by a SOC_ macro.
  4. A feature only one chip has: the C3's built-in temperature sensor is read and shown on the OLED's bottom line and in the Serial line, but only where SOC_TEMP_SENSOR_SUPPORTED is true; on the ESP32 those lines aren't compiled at all.
  5. It compiles for both esp32:esp32:esp32 and esp32:esp32:esp32c3 (Verify with each board selected) and behaves the same on both in Wokwi: the alarm above 35 °C, all clear below 33 °C, the mute button, the OLED.

3. The power budget, one cycle = 15 min = 900 s. Timer wake-up: about 2 s at an average 120 mA with Wi-Fi (6.1's number) = 240 mA·s. Button: say 4 presses a day × 3.2 s × 50 mA (CPU + OLED) = 640 mA·s a day ÷ 96 cycles ≈ 7 mA·s per cycle. Sleep on a low-power build (bare module + HT7833): chip 0.01 + regulator about 0.005 + divider 3.7 V ÷ 40 kΩ = 0.093 + OLED off about 0.01 ≈ 0.12 mA × 898 s ≈ 108 mA·s.

  • I_avg = (240 + 7 + 108) ÷ 900 ≈ 0.39 mA → 1000 ÷ 0.39 ≈ 2560 h ≈ 107 days; realistic (× 0.75) ≈ 80 days. R6 passes.
  • The surprise: the divider is almost a quarter of the whole budget (83 of 355 mA·s). With 2 × 1 MΩ (plus 100 nF from GPIO32 to GND so the ADC reads correctly) it drops to 0.002 mA and the realistic life rises to about 100 days.
  • Without the power pin, a real capacitive soil sensor (about 5 mA) would run all the time: I_avg ≈ 5.4 mA, about 6 days. That one design decision is worth a factor of 14.
  • On a DevKitC (about 6 mA asleep): (240 + 7 + 6 × 898) ÷ 900 ≈ 6.3 mA → about 5 days realistic. The code is the same; the board isn't.

#include <WiFi.h>
#include <PubSubClient.h>

#include <Adafruit_GFX.h>
#include <Adafruit_SSD1306.h>
#include "soc/soc_caps.h"
#if SOC_PM_SUPPORT_EXT0_WAKEUP
  #include "driver/rtc_io.h"
#endif

// ---------- Settings ----------
const char* WIFI_SSID = "Wokwi-GUEST";           // at home: your Wi-Fi name
const char* WIFI_PASS = "";
const char* MQTT_HOST = "broker.hivemq.com";
const char* TOPIC     = "esp32course/yourname/plant";  // make it unique
const uint64_t SLEEP_MIN = 15;                   // 15 minutes (1 while testing)
const uint32_t WIFI_TIMEOUT_MS = 8000;           // never wait longer
const int SOIL_DRY = 3000, SOIL_WET = 1200;      // raw ADC: calibrate your sensor
const int LOW_BAT_MV = 3400;                     // below this: "low battery"

// ---------- Pins: the only part that differs per chip ----------
#if CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32
  const int PIN_SOIL = 34, PIN_PWR = 26, PIN_BAT = 32, PIN_BTN = 27;
  const int PIN_SDA = 21, PIN_SCL = 22;
#elif CONFIG_IDF_TARGET_ESP32C3
  const int PIN_SOIL = 3, PIN_PWR = 10, PIN_BAT = 4, PIN_BTN = 5;
  const int PIN_SDA = 6, PIN_SCL = 7;
#else
  #error "This monitor supports the ESP32 and the ESP32-C3 only"
#endif

Adafruit_SSD1306 oled(128, 64, &Wire, -1);
WiFiClient net;
PubSubClient mqtt(net);

// ---------- RTC memory: survives deep sleep ----------
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint32_t bootCount = 0;
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint32_t failCount = 0;
RTC_DATA_ATTR bool lastSendOk = false;
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint8_t savedChannel = 0;          // Wi-Fi shortcut (lesson 6.1)
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint8_t savedBssid[6];

int readSoilPct() {
  gpio_hold_dis((gpio_num_t)PIN_PWR);            // release last sleep's hold
  pinMode(PIN_PWR, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(PIN_PWR, HIGH);                   // sensor power ON only now
  delay(100);                                    // let the sensor settle
  int raw = analogRead(PIN_SOIL);                // 0..4095
  digitalWrite(PIN_PWR, LOW);                    // and OFF again
  return constrain(map(raw, SOIL_DRY, SOIL_WET, 0, 100), 0, 100);
}

int readBatteryMv() {
  uint32_t sum = 0;
  for (int i = 0; i < 8; i++) sum += analogReadMilliVolts(PIN_BAT);  // average of 8
  return (sum / 8) * 2;                          // the 20k/20k divider halves it
}

bool connectWiFiFast() {
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
  WiFi.persistent(false);
  if (savedChannel != 0) WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS, savedChannel, savedBssid);
  else WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);
  uint32_t start = millis();
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) {
    if (millis() - start > WIFI_TIMEOUT_MS) { savedChannel = 0; return false; }
    delay(20);
  }
  savedChannel = WiFi.channel();
  memcpy(savedBssid, WiFi.BSSID(), 6);
  return true;
}

bool publishReading(int soil, int batMv) {
  if (!connectWiFiFast()) return false;
  mqtt.setServer(MQTT_HOST, 1883);
  String id = "plant-" + WiFi.macAddress() + "-" + String(esp_random() & 0xFFFF, HEX);
  if (!mqtt.connect(id.c_str())) return false;
  char msg[80];
  snprintf(msg, sizeof(msg), "{\"soil\":%d,\"bat\":%d,\"low\":%d,\"n\":%lu,\"fails\":%lu}",
           soil, batMv, batMv < LOW_BAT_MV, (unsigned long)bootCount, (unsigned long)failCount);
  bool ok = mqtt.publish(TOPIC, msg);
  mqtt.loop();
  delay(50);                                     // let the packet leave
  mqtt.disconnect();
  if (ok) Serial.printf("Sent: %s\n", msg);
  return ok;
}

void showOnOled(int soil, int batMv) {
  Wire.begin(PIN_SDA, PIN_SCL);
  if (!oled.begin(SSD1306_SWITCHCAPVCC, 0x3C)) { Serial.println("OLED not found"); return; }
  oled.clearDisplay();
  oled.setTextColor(SSD1306_WHITE);
  oled.setTextSize(2);
  oled.setCursor(0, 0);
  oled.printf("Soil %d%%", soil);
  oled.setCursor(0, 22);
  oled.printf("%.2f V", batMv / 1000.0);
  oled.setTextSize(1);
  oled.setCursor(0, 48);
  oled.print(batMv < LOW_BAT_MV ? "LOW BATTERY! " : "");
  oled.print(lastSendOk ? "last send OK" : "last send FAILED");
  oled.display();
  delay(3000);                                   // the only long wait: the user reads
  oled.ssd1306_command(SSD1306_DISPLAYOFF);      // an OLED left on drains the battery
}

void sleepNow() {
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_OFF);
  gpio_hold_en((gpio_num_t)PIN_PWR);             // sensor power stays LOW in sleep
  gpio_deep_sleep_hold_en();
  pinMode(PIN_BTN, INPUT_PULLUP);
  while (digitalRead(PIN_BTN) == LOW) delay(10); // wait until the button is released
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(SLEEP_MIN * 60ULL * 1000000ULL);
#if SOC_PM_SUPPORT_EXT0_WAKEUP
  esp_sleep_enable_ext0_wakeup((gpio_num_t)PIN_BTN, 0);   // ESP32: EXT0, LOW
  rtc_gpio_pullup_en((gpio_num_t)PIN_BTN);
  rtc_gpio_pulldown_dis((gpio_num_t)PIN_BTN);
#else
  esp_deep_sleep_enable_gpio_wakeup(1ULL << PIN_BTN, ESP_GPIO_WAKEUP_GPIO_LOW);  // C3
#endif
  Serial.printf("Awake %lu ms. Sleep %llu min\n", millis(), SLEEP_MIN);
  Serial.flush();
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  bootCount++;
  analogReadResolution(12);
  int soil = readSoilPct();                      // cheap jobs first
  int batMv = readBatteryMv();
  Serial.printf("#%lu soil=%d %% bat=%d mV\n", (unsigned long)bootCount, soil, batMv);
  esp_sleep_wakeup_cause_t cause = esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause();
  if (cause == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT0 || cause == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_GPIO) {
    showOnOled(soil, batMv);                     // button: show, no Wi-Fi
  } else {                                       // timer or power-on: send
    lastSendOk = publishReading(soil, batMv);
    if (!lastSendOk) { failCount++; Serial.println("Not sent, will retry next wake"); }
  }
  sleepNow();
}

void loop() {}
  1. soc_caps.h, and rtc_io.h only where EXT0 exists. The capability decides, not the chip name: any future chip with EXT0 gets the EXT0 code automatically.
  2. Pins per chip, #error for the rest. The pin table, typed in. The same names in both branches, so the rest of the file never asks which chip it's on.
  3. Soil: power on, settle, read, power off, scale. map turns the dry-to-wet range into 0 to 100, and constrain keeps readings outside the calibration inside that range. The sensor is powered for about 100 ms per wake-up instead of all the time (the factor-14 decision). gpio_hold_dis first, because the pin was locked LOW during sleep.
  4. Battery: 8 calibrated readings, averaged, times 2. analogReadMilliVolts corrects the ESP32's ADC curve; averaging smooths out noise; the divider halved the voltage, so we double it back.
  5. One JSON message. Soil, battery, the low flag, the wake counter and the failure counter, then the usual loop, 50 ms and disconnect. One message = one network round trip = the shortest awake time. fails travels inside the next successful message, so you learn about the bad ones too.
  6. The display path. I2C and the OLED start only here; show for 3 seconds, then SSD1306_DISPLAYOFF. On timer wake-ups the OLED is never touched and stays dark. An OLED left lit would draw several mA all night, more than everything else together.
  7. Hold the sensor pin LOW and wait for the button. Without the hold the pin could float in sleep and half-power the sensor; without the release wait, a held button would wake the board again at once.
  8. Two wake sources, per capability. The timer always; EXT0 with the RTC pull-up where the chip has it, the C3's GPIO wake-up otherwise. This is the only place the two chips really differ. On the C3, pinMode(PIN_BTN, INPUT_PULLUP) on line 114 provides the pull-up; if your C3 board wakes up by itself, add an external 10 kΩ from GPIO5 to 3V3.
  9. Cheap jobs first. Read and print before deciding anything. Both paths need the numbers, and reading them costs about 100 ms, a tiny fraction of a Wi-Fi connection.
  10. The dispatcher. A button (EXT0 on the ESP32, GPIO on the C3) shows; anything else (timer, power-on) sends. Power-on sends right away on purpose: when you put the battery in, you see the first message in your client within seconds, so you know the installation works.

Compile check: esp32:esp32:esp32 OK and esp32:esp32:esp32c3 OK with core 3.3.12 (R7).

5. Measurements (example numbers from a DevKitC on the bench; yours will differ): awake about 1.5 s on timer wake-ups after the first, 3.2 s on button wake-ups; sleep current 5.8 mA with the OLED off. Forgetting SSD1306_DISPLAYOFF added several mA more in a test. Recomputed: (120 × 1.5 + 7 + 5.8 × 898.5) ÷ 900 ≈ 6.0 mA → about 5 days realistic on the DevKitC; the low-power build is needed for R6.
