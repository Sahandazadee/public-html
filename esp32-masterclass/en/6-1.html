Energy from zero: current, milliamps and milliamp-hours

Before any code, we need to understand how a battery "runs out". There are two important numbers:

  • Current: how much electricity the circuit is drawing right now. It is measured in amps (A). The ESP32 uses far less than one amp, so we usually talk in milliamps (mA, a thousandth of an amp) and microamps (µA, a millionth of an amp). So 1 mA = 1000 µA.
  • Battery capacity: how much "stored electricity" the battery holds in total. It is measured in milliamp-hours (mAh). A 2000 mAh battery can supply 2000 mA for one hour, or 100 mA for 20 hours, or 1 mA for 2000 hours.
An everyday example: a water tank

Picture the battery as a 2000-liter water tank. Current is how much water flows out of the tap each hour. Open the tap all the way (100 liters per hour) and the tank is empty in 20 hours. Leave only a drip open (1 liter per hour) and the tank lasts 2000 hours, which is almost 3 months. The whole art of this lesson is keeping the ESP32's tap on "drip" most of the time.

Work it out: an ESP32 that is always on

A simple formula: life (hours) = capacity (mAh) ÷ current (mA)

With Wi-Fi on and doing work, the ESP32 draws somewhere around 80 to 240 mA on average, depending on the job. If we call the average 120 mA and use a 2000 mAh battery:

2000 ÷ 120 ≈ 16.7 h, which is less than a day! 😬

In practice it is even less, because a battery never runs all the way down to zero (the circuit shuts off before that) and the regulator wastes a little as well. A cautious rule of thumb: count only about 70 to 80 percent of the capacity on the label as usable.

Average current and duty cycle

The solution is to let the ESP32 sleep most of the time and wake up for just a few seconds. Then there is no longer a single constant current; we have to take a weighted average. The formula:

The average-current formula

I_avg = (I_awake × t_awake + I_sleep × t_sleep) ÷ (t_awake + t_sleep)

In words: awake current times awake time, plus sleep current times sleep time, divided by the length of one full cycle. The ratio t_awake ÷ (t_awake + t_sleep) is called the duty cycle: the percentage of time the device is awake.

Three scenarios with a 2000 mAh battery (waking once every 10 minutes)

One cycle = 10 minutes = 600 seconds.

Scenario A: bare chip, 5 seconds awake at 120 mA, deep sleep the rest of the time at 0.01 mA (10 µA):
(120 × 5 + 0.01 × 595) ÷ 600 = (600 + 5.95) ÷ 600 ≈ 1.01 mA
2000 ÷ 1.01 ≈ 1980 h ≈ 82 days

Scenario B: the same code, but on an ordinary development board that still draws about 8 mA while asleep:
(120 × 5 + 8 × 595) ÷ 600 = (600 + 4760) ÷ 600 ≈ 8.9 mA
2000 ÷ 8.9 ≈ 224 h ≈ 9 days, not 82 days!

Scenario C: bare chip, but we've cut the awake time to 1.5 seconds with the tricks from this lesson:
(120 × 1.5 + 0.01 × 598.5) ÷ 600 = (180 + 5.99) ÷ 600 ≈ 0.31 mA
2000 ÷ 0.31 ≈ 6450 h ≈ 269 days

Two big lessons from these three lines: (1) the board's sleep current can ruin everything; (2) once the sleep current is low, the awake time is the most important number. (These calculations ignore the battery's self-discharge and the capacity it loses in the cold, so the real numbers are lower.)

Why "the area under the graph"?

The peaks are tall but very narrow. Sleep is short (in height) but very wide. Energy = height × width. That is why, in scenario B, the low but wide orange line ate more battery than all the peaks put together.

The ESP32 has several power modes. The lower you go, the more parts are switched off and the more limited the ways to wake up become. An everyday example: an office worker taking a break

Modem-sleep: the worker is at the desk but has hung up the phone. Light sleep: head down on the desk, taking a nap; all the papers are still open in front of them, and when they wake up they carry on from exactly the same sentence. Deep sleep: they went home to bed; the desk has been cleared and tomorrow they must start the job from scratch, with only a small note left in their pocket (RTC memory). Hibernation: even the note in their pocket has been thrown away; only the alarm clock is still running.

The hard truth: a dev board does not draw "10 microamps" asleep

The 10 µA figure is for the ESP32 chip itself. But you have a development board, and it carries other parts that never go to sleep:

  • The voltage regulator: most cheap boards use an AMS1117 regulator, which, according to its datasheet, typically uses about 5 mA (and up to 10 mA) just to keep itself running. This is called its quiescent current.
  • The USB-to-serial chip (CP2102 or CH340): on many boards it is powered from the same supply rail even when no USB cable is plugged in.
  • The power LED: that little red LED alone can draw one to several milliamps.

The result: a typical development board in deep sleep draws anywhere from about one to more than ten milliamps, depending on the model. That is hundreds of times the datasheet number. The only way to know the figure for your board is to measure it. For a real battery-powered product, either use boards designed for batteries (a low-power regulator, no always-on LED) or put an ESP32 module on your own board with a suitable regulator (see the PCB design lessons in the KiCad tutorial).

Don't be fooled by the code alone

Your sleep code can be completely correct and the battery can still be dead in a week. That doesn't mean the code has a problem; it means the hardware does. Until you have measured, make no promises about battery life.

Batteries for beginners: lithium cells, regulators, chargers

(You saw the basics of powering a circuit, regulators and the "common ground" rule in the power section of lesson 1.4; here we only add the points that are specific to batteries and sleep.) The most popular battery for the ESP32 is a single-cell lithium battery: either a LiPo (a flat pouch, usually with a JST connector) or a cylindrical 18650 cell. Both have a nominal voltage of 3.7 V: 4.2 V when full and about 3.0 V when nearly empty.

Don't connect the battery straight to 3V3

The board's 3V3 pin goes straight to the ESP32, and the ESP32 can only tolerate up to about 3.6 V. A full battery is 4.2 V, so connecting it directly can damage the chip. Connecting it to the 5V pin isn't a good idea either: the AMS1117 regulator on the board needs about one volt of headroom, so with a 3.7 V input its output drops below 3 V and the board resets the moment Wi-Fi transmits (a brownout). On top of that, it still has its own quiescent draw of several milliamps.

The right way is a low-power LDO regulator with two properties: (1) a very low quiescent current, just a few microamps, and (2) the ability to supply Wi-Fi's current bursts (a few hundred milliamps) with a small voltage drop.

RegulatorMaximum currentQuiescent current (per datasheet)Notes

MCP1700-3302250 mAAbout 1.6 µAExcellent quiescent current, but 250 mA is borderline for Wi-Fi peaks; a large capacitor (e.g. 470 µF) on the output is required, and the board may still reset
What does dropout mean?

To give a steady 3.3 V, every regulator needs its input to be a little above 3.3 V. That gap is called the "dropout voltage". With a good LDO, the output stays at a proper 3.3 V down to a battery voltage of about 3.5 V; below that, the output sags a little. The ESP32 usually keeps working down to about 3 V, but Wi-Fi becomes unstable at low voltages. Always read the datasheet of the regulator you buy.

The charger: a TP4056 module

Charge a lithium battery only with a charger made for lithium cells. The cheapest option is a TP4056 module, which runs from USB and charges the battery up to 4.2 V. It comes in two versions: charger only, and a version with protection (it has a DW01 chip with transistors next to it, and separate OUT+ and OUT− outputs). Always buy the version with protection, and connect the load (the regulator and the ESP32) to OUT+ and OUT−, not straight to the battery.

Work it out: the charging current

On a TP4056 module, a resistor called R_PROG sets the charging current. According to the datasheet it is roughly I = 1200 ÷ R_PROG (current in amps, resistance in ohms). Typical modules have 1.2 kΩ, which means 1 A. The safety rule: don't charge faster than 1C, the battery's capacity number. For a 500 mAh battery, 1 A is 2C and dangerous; you need to change R_PROG to about 2.4 kΩ so the current becomes 500 mA.

Lithium battery safety is no joke
  • Set aside any battery that is swollen, punctured, crushed or hot right away; never charge it.
  • Never short-circuit the terminals, not even for a moment. A lithium battery delivers a very large current that can make a wire red-hot and melt it.
  • Check the polarity of the JST connector before plugging it in. Different manufacturers put the red and black wires in the connector the opposite way round; plugging it in reversed burns out the charger or the board.
  • Do your first few charges under supervision on a fireproof surface (a tile or a metal tray, for example), not on a bed or a carpet.
  • Don't use an "unprotected" 18650 cell without a protection circuit.
  • Don't leave a battery in direct sunlight or in a hot car.

Measuring current with a multimeter (safely)

If you're not yet comfortable with your multimeter's sockets and selector dial, first review the multimeter section in lesson 1.4; here we only add current measurement.

You don't need a battery to measure current. Two ways, from easiest to most precise:

  • A USB power meter (a small "USB tester" that plugs in between the USB charger or computer and the board's cable; they cost a few dollars). It shows volts and milliamps directly, with no wiring at all. Most of them only resolve 1 to 10 mA, so they're great for the awake current and for seeing the difference between "awake" and "asleep" on a dev board, but too coarse for microamps.
  • Your multimeter in series with the 5V line, with no extra parts. The board you measure gets its 5 V through the multimeter instead of through its own USB socket. The 5 V comes from your second ESP32 board (the one from lesson 5.2) plugged into USB: its 5V pin carries the USB 5 V. Any other 5 V USB source with pins works too. The wiring and the steps are below.
Why in series? A water meter

A water meter is fitted in the middle of the pipe so that all the water flows through it and gets counted. An ammeter works the same way: you have to cut the pipe (the wire) and put the ammeter in the middle. If you put the ammeter "beside" the battery (in parallel), it's like connecting the pipe straight to the drain: the ammeter has almost zero resistance, so you are effectively short-circuiting the battery. The result: the multimeter's fuse blows and a lithium battery may get hot.

  1. Upload first, then unplug. Upload the sleep program to the board you want to measure, then unplug its USB cable. The program stays in flash; from now on only the 5 V line through the multimeter powers it.
  2. Move the multimeter's red lead to the current socket. Start with the 10A socket (or the largest range) and turn the selector to DC current (the A with a straight line). Always start with the largest range, then go down.
  3. Put the multimeter in the gap: the source's 5V pin → red probe, black probe (COM) → the measured board's 5V pin. Then connect the two GND pins with a plain jumper wire.
  4. Read the awake current (tens of milliamps, with brief jumps). Most multimeters are far too slow to catch the short Wi-Fi peaks; the number on the display is only an approximation.
  5. Once the board is asleep, lower the range to mA for a more precise reading. On a DevKitC, expect a few milliamps even in deep sleep: that's the regulator and the USB chip from the section above, not your code.
  6. Finished? Always move the red lead back to the voltage (V) socket. Otherwise your next mistake will be measuring the battery voltage with the lead still in the current socket, which is a short circuit.

Measuring a battery build. Once you've built the battery chain from the section above, you measure it the same way, with the multimeter between the battery's positive lead and the regulator's input. Here the µA range becomes useful, because a good LDO and a bare module really can sleep in the microamp range:

The "burden voltage" trap on the microamp range

To measure current, the multimeter puts a small resistor in the path. On the µA range that resistor is fairly large. When the ESP32 wakes up and suddenly draws tens of milliamps, the voltage across that resistor rises, and the board may reset (brownout) and get stuck in an endless loop of waking up and resetting. If you see this, the problem is the multimeter, not your code: read the sleep current on the µA range and switch back to the mA range before the next wake-up, or use a dedicated current-measuring tool (a power profiler).

Deep sleep with a timer wake-up

The simplest and most common method: "go to sleep and wake up in 10 seconds". The most important point, one to remember forever: after deep sleep, the program starts over from the beginning, just as if you had pressed the EN button. So in this kind of program loop() never runs at all, and all the work happens in setup().

// Deep sleep with a timer wake-up
const uint64_t uS_PER_SEC = 1000000ULL;   // microseconds in one second
const uint64_t SLEEP_SEC  = 10;           // wake up every 10 seconds

RTC_DATA_ATTR int bootCount = 0;          // in RTC memory: survives sleep
int normalCount = 0;                      // in normal RAM: reset to zero every time

void printWakeReason() {
  esp_sleep_wakeup_cause_t cause = esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause();
  switch (cause) {
    case ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_TIMER:    Serial.println("Wake: timer"); break;
    case ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT0:     Serial.println("Wake: EXT0 (one pin)"); break;
    case ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT1:     Serial.println("Wake: EXT1 (several pins)"); break;
    case ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_TOUCHPAD: Serial.println("Wake: touch"); break;
    default: Serial.println("Wake: power-on or reset (not from sleep)"); break;
  }
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(500);                             // time for the Serial Monitor to open
  bootCount++;
  normalCount++;
  Serial.printf("bootCount=%d  normalCount=%d\n", bootCount, normalCount);
  printWakeReason();

  // ... the real work goes here: read sensors, send data ...

  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(SLEEP_SEC * uS_PER_SEC);
  Serial.println("Going to deep sleep for 10 s...");
  Serial.flush();                         // wait until all the text has been sent
  esp_deep_sleep_start();                 // nothing after this runs
  Serial.println("This line never runs");
}

void loop() {
  // never runs: after waking up, the program starts from setup
}
  1. Sleep time in microseconds. The sleep timer function takes its time in microseconds. 10 seconds is 10 million microseconds. The ULL suffix means "unsigned 64-bit number" (unsigned long long). A 32-bit number tops out at about 4.29 billion, which is only about 71 minutes in microseconds. If you write 3600 * 1000000 (one hour) without ULL, the multiplication overflows in 32 bits and the board sleeps for a completely wrong time. uint64_t and ULL remove that danger.
  2. RTC_DATA_ATTR. This tag tells the compiler to put the variable in the small RTC memory (about 8 KB), which stays powered during deep sleep, instead of in normal RAM. Because the main RAM is switched off in deep sleep, this is the only simple way to "remember" a number between two wake-ups: a counter, the last temperature, an alarm state. But a power cut or the EN button wipes it too.
  3. A normal variable for comparison. normalCount lives in ordinary RAM. In the output you'll see that bootCount goes up every time, but normalCount is always 1. This experiment proves the "starts from the beginning" idea with your own eyes.
  4. Why did I wake up? esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() returns the wake-up cause: the timer, a pin (EXT0 or EXT1), touch, or none of them (meaning the power was just connected or the board was reset). Since every wake-up starts from setup, this is the only way for the program to tell "this is the normal timer wake-up" from "the user pressed the button" and behave differently in each case.
  5. The awake work. The counters go up and get printed. The delay(500) is only for testing. Remove this delay in a real project; you saw in the scenarios above how expensive every extra half second awake is.
  6. Setting the wake-up source. esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(time) sets the RTC timer's alarm. Without any wake-up source, esp_deep_sleep_start() puts the board to sleep forever (until a reset or power cut). Note that the RTC timer runs from an internal oscillator and can be off by a few percent; that is fine for "every 10 minutes", but not for an accurate clock.
  7. Serial.flush(). Waits until every character in the queue has actually left the TX pin. Without it, the board may fall asleep in the middle of sending the last line, and you see it cut off or garbled in the Serial Monitor.
  8. Going to sleep. After esp_deep_sleep_start() no line runs; line 33 is there on purpose and should never be printed. If one day you do see this line in the output, sleep never started (for example because a wake-up source was set up wrongly). A small trap for troubleshooting.
  9. An empty loop. It has to exist because Arduino requires it, but we never reach it. This is the standard pattern for every "wake up, work, sleep" program.
bootCount=1 normalCount=1 Wake: power-on or reset (not from sleep) Going to deep sleep for 10 s... ets Jun 8 2016 00:22:57 rst:0x5 (DEEPSLEEP_RESET),boot:0x13 (SPI_FAST_FLASH_BOOT) ... bootCount=2 normalCount=1 Wake: timer Going to deep sleep for 10 s...

The line rst:0x5 (DEEPSLEEP_RESET) is the ESP32's own message when it wakes up, and it proves the board really came back from deep sleep and booted from the beginning.

This circuit has no wiring; it's just the board. Open an empty ESP32 project in Wokwi, paste the code and run it. Deep sleep with a timer works in Wokwi, and you'll see the repeated boots in the Serial Monitor. But remember that Wokwi does not show current draw; you only see the real savings with a multimeter on a real board.

Waking up with a button (EXT0)

Sometimes you don't want the board to wake up every few minutes; you want it to wake up only when someone presses a button (like a doorbell or a remote control). For that we use EXT0: one specific pin at one specific level (LOW or HIGH) wakes the board.

Only RTC pins can wake the board

In deep sleep only the RTC part has power, so only pins connected to the RTC (RTC GPIOs) can wake the board. On the ESP32 these are: 0, 2, 4, 12–15, 25–27, 32–39. We use GPIO27, the course's usual button pin (so it's already wired), because it is an RTC pin, it is not a strapping pin, and it has internal pull-up and pull-down resistors. (GPIO34 to 39 have no internal resistors, and GPIO0, 2, 12 and 15 are strapping pins.)

// Waking up with a button (EXT0) on GPIO27
#include "driver/rtc_io.h"

const gpio_num_t WAKE_PIN = GPIO_NUM_27;     // must be an RTC pin
RTC_DATA_ATTR int bootCount = 0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(500);
  bootCount++;
  Serial.printf("Boot #%d\n", bootCount);

  if (esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT0) {
    Serial.println("Woken up by the button!");
  }

  // the button is between GPIO27 and GND: pressed = LOW
  esp_sleep_enable_ext0_wakeup(WAKE_PIN, 0);   // 0 means wake up on LOW
  rtc_gpio_pullup_en(WAKE_PIN);                // internal RTC pull-up on
  rtc_gpio_pulldown_dis(WAKE_PIN);             // pull-down off

  Serial.println("Sleeping until button press...");
  Serial.flush();
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void loop() {}
  1. The rtc_io header. The rtc_gpio_... functions are defined in this ESP-IDF header file. In deep sleep the pins are no longer controlled by pinMode (that part is switched off); the RTC part controls them. So the pull-up resistor has to be switched on through the RTC as well.
  2. The gpio_num_t type. The sleep functions want a gpio_num_t instead of a plain number; GPIO_NUM_27 is simply pin 27. The compiler won't accept a plain number (an int) in its place, so you can't accidentally pass some unrelated number or variable where a pin belongs.
  3. Detecting a button wake-up. If the wake-up cause was EXT0, a special message is printed. In a real project this is where the main work happens: for example, sending a "someone rang the doorbell" message.
  4. esp_sleep_enable_ext0_wakeup(pin, level). Level 0 means "wake up when the pin goes LOW"; 1 means HIGH. The button is connected to GND, so pressing it pulls the pin LOW. If you connect the button to 3V3 instead, set the level to 1 and switch on the pull-down instead of the pull-up.
  5. The RTC pull-up. The pin is normally held HIGH and the pull-down is switched off. Without a resistor the pin is floating, and nearby electrical noise might pull it LOW and wake the board for no reason. In EXT0 mode, the part that powers these resistors automatically stays on.
  6. Sleep. Just like before: message, flush, sleep. If the button is still held down when this line runs, the board wakes up again immediately. In a real project, wait until the button is released before sleeping.

In Wokwi, press the button after the Sleeping until button press... message. Wokwi simulates the RTC pull-up and pull-down resistors. If the simulator ever behaves differently from what you expect, the real board has the final say.

Waking up with several buttons (EXT1)

EXT0 watches only one pin. If you have two or more buttons (for example "button A: send now" and "button B: settings mode"), use EXT1. EXT1 takes a bit mask of pins, and after waking up it also tells you which pin caused the wake-up.

An important trap: the original ESP32 has only two EXT1 modes

On the original ESP32 chip (the one on this DevKitC) there are only two modes: ESP_EXT1_WAKEUP_ANY_HIGH (any of the pins goes HIGH) and ESP_EXT1_WAKEUP_ALL_LOW (all the pins are LOW at the same time). The ESP_EXT1_WAKEUP_ANY_LOW mode is defined only for newer chips such as the ESP32-S2, S3, C6 and H2; we checked this directly in the esp_sleep.h file of ESP-IDF version 5 (the base of core 3.x). If you see ANY_LOW used for a plain ESP32 in a tutorial or an AI answer, you'll hit a "was not declared in this scope" error. That's why in this example we connect the buttons to 3V3 and watch for "any of them goes HIGH".

// Waking up with either of two buttons (EXT1)
const int BTN_A = 32;
const int BTN_B = 33;
const uint64_t WAKE_MASK = (1ULL << BTN_A) | (1ULL << BTN_B);

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(500);

  if (esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT1) {
    uint64_t st = esp_sleep_get_ext1_wakeup_status();   // which pins?
    if (st & (1ULL << BTN_A)) Serial.println("Button A (GPIO32) woke me");
    if (st & (1ULL << BTN_B)) Serial.println("Button B (GPIO33) woke me");
  } else {
    Serial.println("Normal start");
  }

  // the buttons go to 3V3 and have 10k to GND: pressed = HIGH
  esp_sleep_enable_ext1_wakeup(WAKE_MASK, ESP_EXT1_WAKEUP_ANY_HIGH);
  Serial.println("Sleeping. Press A or B...");
  Serial.flush();
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void loop() {}
  1. A bit mask. Each pin is one bit in a 64-bit number. 1ULL << 32 means "shift the number 1 left by 32 places": a number whose only 1 bit is bit 32. With | (bitwise OR) we switch on both bits together. The ESP32 has more than 32 pins, so a 32-bit number isn't enough. If you write 1 << 32 (without ULL), the behavior is undefined and the mask comes out wrong; it's one of the classic bugs.
  2. Which button woke me? esp_sleep_get_ext1_wakeup_status() returns a mask in which the bits of the pins that caused the wake-up are 1. With & (bitwise AND) we check each button's bit separately. This means a sleeping board can do different jobs for different buttons, without having to read the pins again after waking up (by which time the button may already have been released).
  3. Enabling EXT1. We pass the mask and the ANY_HIGH mode. Core 3.x also has a newer name (esp_sleep_enable_ext1_wakeup_io), which the latest official examples use; the older name used here works in every 3.x version. It may be removed in ESP-IDF version 6.
  4. Sleep. Same as the previous examples. We fitted external pull-down resistors. Unlike EXT0, EXT1 does not keep the part that powers the internal RTC resistors switched on by default, so external resistors are the most reliable choice for a beginner.

Two buttons and two 10 kΩ resistors. After the Sleeping message, press either button and see which one gets reported.

Waking up with touch

The ESP32 has several touch pins that work without any button: you just touch a wire or a piece of aluminum foil. These pins can wake the board from deep sleep too. We pick T9, which is GPIO32, because it is not a strapping pin, and because GPIO32 already has the course's push button on it. (GPIO32 is the course's swappable pin for a servo or NeoPixel; neither is used in this lesson.)

// Waking up by touch on GPIO32 (T9)
const int THRESHOLD = 40;   // on the ESP32: a touch reading below this = touched

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(500);
  if (esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_TOUCHPAD) {
    Serial.println("Woken up by touch!");
  }
  Serial.printf("touchRead(T9) now = %d\n", touchRead(T9));
  touchSleepWakeUpEnable(T9, THRESHOLD);   // core 3.x API
  Serial.println("Touch the wire on GPIO32 to wake me");
  Serial.flush();
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void loop() {}
  1. The touch threshold. On the original ESP32, touchRead gives a larger number when nothing is touching the pin, and the number drops when you touch it. 40 is the value Espressif's official example uses for the ESP32. The right number depends on the wire length, the size of the foil and even the humidity. That's why line 10 prints the current value, so you can tune the threshold yourself: usually somewhere between the "not touched" and "touched" numbers. (On the ESP32-S2 and S3 it's the other way round: touching makes the number bigger.)
  2. Detecting a touch wake-up. The cause is ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_TOUCHPAD. As with the other sources, after waking up we start from setup and need to find out why.
  3. touchSleepWakeUpEnable(touch pin, threshold). Registers pin T9 as a wake-up source. This function was added in core 2.0.6 and is the recommended way in 3.x. Older tutorials use touchAttachInterrupt(T9, callback, Threshold) followed by esp_sleep_enable_touchpad_wakeup().
Wokwi and touch

Holding a pin's state during sleep

Say you use a pin to drive a transistor that switches the power of a power-hungry sensor on and off. When the ESP32 goes to sleep, its pins may be released and the transistor could switch on by accident. The gpio_hold_en function "locks" a pin's state.

// Holding an output pin's state during deep sleep
const gpio_num_t PWR_PIN = GPIO_NUM_26;   // e.g. the control pin of the sensor's power transistor

void goToSleep() {
  digitalWrite(PWR_PIN, LOW);             // the state we want: sensor power off
  gpio_hold_en(PWR_PIN);                  // lock this state
  gpio_deep_sleep_hold_en();              // keep the lock during deep sleep too
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(60 * 1000000ULL);
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void setup() {
  gpio_hold_dis(PWR_PIN);                 // after waking up, release the lock first
  pinMode(PWR_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(PWR_PIN, HIGH);            // sensor power on
  delay(100);                             // ... read the sensor ...
  goToSleep();
}

void loop() {}
  1. Locking the pin. First we write the state we want, then gpio_hold_en locks it. On the ESP32, gpio_deep_sleep_hold_en turns on the lock for digital pins during deep sleep as well. GPIO26 is an RTC pin, and for RTC pins gpio_hold_en alone is enough (it is equivalent to rtc_gpio_hold_en). Line 7 is needed for non-RTC pins, and having it here does no harm.
  2. Releasing the lock after waking up. If you don't release it, every later digitalWrite on this pin does nothing, and you can spend hours hunting for a bug that isn't in your code!

Light sleep: a short nap, then carry on

In light sleep, the RAM stays powered. So after waking up, the program continues from the line after esp_light_sleep_start(), and all your ordinary variables are still where you left them. The price: about 0.8 mA instead of 10 µA. Light sleep suits short pauses (from a fraction of a second to a few seconds) and programs that you don't want to restart from the beginning.

Another advantage: to wake from light sleep with a pin, the pin doesn't have to be an RTC pin; any GPIO input works with gpio_wakeup_enable.

// Light sleep: the program carries on from where it stopped
#include "driver/gpio.h"

const int BTN = 27;            // button between GPIO27 and GND
int counter = 0;               // an ordinary variable; not wiped in light sleep

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(BTN, INPUT_PULLUP);
  gpio_wakeup_enable((gpio_num_t)BTN, GPIO_INTR_LOW_LEVEL);  // LOW = wake up
  esp_sleep_enable_gpio_wakeup();
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(5 * 1000000ULL);             // or after 5 seconds
}

void loop() {
  counter++;
  Serial.printf("Awake! counter=%d\n", counter);
  Serial.flush();                        // send all the text before sleeping
  esp_light_sleep_start();               // it naps here ...
  // ... and after waking up it carries on from this very line
  esp_sleep_wakeup_cause_t c = esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause();
  Serial.println(c == ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_GPIO ? "Wake: button" : "Wake: timer");
  delay(300);                            // pause so the button can be released
}
  1. An ordinary counter. This time RTC_DATA_ATTR isn't needed. In light sleep the RAM stays on. In the output you'll see counter keep going up; compare that with normalCount in the deep sleep example, which was always 1.
  2. Waking up with a GPIO. gpio_wakeup_enable sets which pin wakes the board at which level, and esp_sleep_enable_gpio_wakeup switches this kind of wake-up on. GPIO wake-up works on the "level", not the "edge". That means that as long as the button is held down, the wake-up condition is true. That's why we wait a little on line 23.
  3. Two wake-up sources at once. The 5-second timer is enabled too; whichever happens first wakes the board. Combining sources is very common: "send a report every hour, but right away if the button is pressed".
  4. esp_light_sleep_start(). The board sleeps, and after waking up, execution continues with the following lines (line 20 onward). This is the biggest difference from deep sleep: no reset happens at all. Note that the Wi-Fi connection is not kept during a light sleep you start yourself with this function; if Wi-Fi is on, turn it off before sleeping, or use ESP-IDF's automatic power management features, which are beyond the scope of this lesson.
  5. Who woke me up? A cause of ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_GPIO means the button; otherwise it was the timer. The same pattern as with deep sleep, but now in the middle of loop.
Why doesn't this example have a Wokwi block?

In Wokwi's official feature table, the RTC part is marked only as "partial", and nothing is promised about how light sleep behaves. Rather than confuse you with a simulation that may behave differently, try this example on a real board.

Project: a battery-powered temperature and humidity logger

Now we put everything together: wake up every 10 minutes, read the DHT22, send the reading over Wi-Fi and MQTT, and go straight back to sleep. The design goal: the shortest possible awake time. Judge every line of this program by one question: "Does this keep the board awake longer?"

// Battery-powered logger: wake up, read, send, sleep
#include <WiFi.h>
#include <PubSubClient.h>
#include <DHT.h>

// ---------- settings ----------
const char* WIFI_SSID = "Wokwi-GUEST";           // at home: your router's network name
const char* WIFI_PASS = "";
const char* MQTT_HOST = "broker.hivemq.com";
const char* TOPIC     = "esp32fa/lesson61/logger"; // make up a unique name of your own
const uint64_t SLEEP_MIN = 10;                     // every 10 minutes
const uint32_t WIFI_TIMEOUT_MS = 8000;             // longest time to try Wi-Fi

#define DHT_PIN  13
#define DHT_TYPE DHT22
DHT dht(DHT_PIN, DHT_TYPE);
WiFiClient net;
PubSubClient mqtt(net);

// ---------- RTC memory: survives deep sleep ----------
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint32_t bootCount = 0;
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint8_t  savedChannel = 0;           // last time's Wi-Fi channel
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint8_t  savedBssid[6];              // last time's router MAC
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint32_t failCount = 0;              // number of failed sends

void goToSleep() {
  WiFi.disconnect(true);                           // switch Wi-Fi fully off
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_OFF);
  Serial.printf("Awake %lu ms, fails=%lu. Sleep %llu min\n", millis(), failCount, SLEEP_MIN);
  Serial.flush();
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(SLEEP_MIN * 60ULL * 1000000ULL);
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

bool connectWiFiFast() {
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
  WiFi.persistent(false);                          // don't write to flash every time
  if (savedChannel != 0) {
    WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS, savedChannel, savedBssid);  // shortcut
  } else {
    WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);              // first time: full scan
  }
  uint32_t start = millis();
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) {
    if (millis() - start > WIFI_TIMEOUT_MS) {
      savedChannel = 0;                            // shortcut failed; do a full scan next time
      return false;
    }
    delay(20);
  }
  savedChannel = WiFi.channel();                   // keep for next time
  memcpy(savedBssid, WiFi.BSSID(), 6);
  return true;
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  bootCount++;
  dht.begin();
  if (esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() != ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_TIMER) delay(2000);  // first power-up: DHT22 not ready yet

  float t = dht.readTemperature();
  float h = dht.readHumidity();
  if (isnan(t) || isnan(h)) {
    Serial.println("DHT22 read failed");
    failCount++;
    goToSleep();                                   // don't switch Wi-Fi on for nothing
  }
  Serial.printf("#%lu  T=%.1f C  H=%.1f %%\n", bootCount, t, h);

  if (connectWiFiFast()) {
    mqtt.setServer(MQTT_HOST, 1883);
    String id = "esp32-logger-" + String((uint32_t)ESP.getEfuseMac(), HEX) + "-" + String(esp_random() & 0xFFFF, HEX);
    if (mqtt.connect(id.c_str())) {
      char msg[64];
      snprintf(msg, sizeof(msg), "{\"t\":%.1f,\"h\":%.1f,\"n\":%lu}", t, h, bootCount);
      mqtt.publish(TOPIC, msg);
      mqtt.loop();
      delay(50);                                   // a moment for the packet to leave
      mqtt.disconnect();
      Serial.println(String("Sent: ") + msg);
    } else {
      failCount++;
    }
  } else {
    failCount++;
    Serial.println("Wi-Fi failed, will retry next wake");
  }
  goToSleep();
}

void loop() {}
  1. Libraries. WiFi.h is part of the core. Install PubSubClient (by Nick O'Leary) and DHT sensor library (by Adafruit, which also needs Adafruit Unified Sensor) from the Library Manager. These are the same libraries we used in lessons 3.3 and 5.1; there's nothing new to learn here except how to sleep.
  2. All the settings in one place. The network name, the MQTT server, the topic, the sleep time and the longest time to keep trying Wi-Fi. WIFI_TIMEOUT_MS is vital: if the router is switched off and the board waits forever for Wi-Fi, it drains the battery in one night. "Never wait forever" is rule number one for battery-powered devices. Be sure to change the topic to a name of your own, because broker.hivemq.com is public and anyone can see the messages.
  3. RTC memory for the Wi-Fi shortcut. Besides the counters, we keep last time's channel and BSSID (the router's MAC address). A normal Wi-Fi connection first scans every channel to find the router, and that scan can take a few seconds. If we already know the channel and the router's address, we go straight to it. Wokwi's documentation recommends passing the channel number for the same reason.
  4. The sleep function. It switches Wi-Fi fully off, reports how long the board was awake (millis()) and sleeps for 10 minutes. 60ULL * 1000000ULL converts minutes to microseconds. Printing millis() right before sleeping gives you the most important number for optimizing: "how many milliseconds was I awake this round?" With it you can see the effect of every change as a number. Having one clean call for sleeping from anywhere in the program means no path gets forgotten.
  5. Fast Wi-Fi connection. If we have a saved channel, WiFi.begin(ssid, pass, channel, bssid) connects using the shortcut; otherwise it connects the normal way. The waiting loop lasts at most 8 seconds. After a successful connection, the channel and BSSID are saved. If the shortcut fails (for example because the router changed its channel), savedChannel = 0 makes the next attempt do a full scan. WiFi.persistent(false) stops the settings being written to flash memory on every wake-up; flash can only be written a limited number of times, and writing also costs time and energy. This is the one place where a short delay inside a loop is acceptable, because there is nothing to do but wait.
  6. Wait 2 seconds, but only on the first power-up. If the wake-up cause is not the timer (meaning the battery or USB was just connected, or you pressed EN), we wait 2 seconds. According to its datasheet, the DHT22 isn't ready for about 1 to 2 seconds after power is applied, but the ESP32 boots in under half a second. Without this line, the first reading on a real board usually returns nan, the board sleeps for 10 minutes, and you think something is broken. On later (timer) wake-ups the sensor has already had power for a while, so the wait isn't needed and costs no battery life.
  7. Sensor first, then Wi-Fi. If reading the DHT22 fails, we go to sleep without switching Wi-Fi on. Wi-Fi is the most expensive part. Switching it on to send "nothing" just wastes battery. Always order your tasks so that the cheap ones that might fail come first.
  8. Connecting to MQTT and sending one JSON message. A unique client ID is built from the MAC address plus a random number (in Wokwi, many students' boards can share the same MAC). The message looks like {"t":24.5,"h":40.0,"n":12}. One JSON message instead of three separate ones means one network round trip instead of three: a shorter awake time. The boot number n helps you spot on your dashboard whether a message went missing.
  9. Time for the packet to leave, then disconnect. mqtt.loop() and a 50-millisecond pause give the packet a chance to actually leave the board; then the connection is closed properly. If you sleep right after publish, the packet is sometimes still sitting in the network queue and never reaches the server. 50 milliseconds is usually enough on a healthy connection; if messages go missing, raise this number a little.
  10. Handling failure. Every failure is counted, and we go to sleep no matter what. A battery-powered device shouldn't stay awake to "try again". The next round will try again by itself. failCount tells you how reliable the network is.
Optional trick: a static IP

After connecting to Wi-Fi, the board gets an IP address from the router (DHCP), which takes a little time. On your own home network you can give it a fixed IP so this step is skipped. Put these lines in connectWiFiFast() just before WiFi.begin (don't do this in Wokwi, because its network has its own addresses):

IPAddress ip(192, 168, 1, 50);        // a free IP, outside the router's DHCP range
IPAddress gateway(192, 168, 1, 1);    // the router's IP
IPAddress subnet(255, 255, 255, 0);
IPAddress dns(8, 8, 8, 8);
WiFi.config(ip, gateway, subnet, dns); // before WiFi.begin
  1. Four addresses. The board's own IP, the router's IP (the gateway), the subnet mask, and a DNS server that turns the name broker.hivemq.com into an IP. Take the numbers from your own router's settings. If you pick an IP that another device already has, both devices will have problems; that's why you choose an IP outside the range the router hands out automatically.
  2. WiFi.config. Applies these settings before connecting. The saving is usually anywhere from a fraction of a second to a second or two, depending on the router. Measure it yourself by printing millis(); if it makes no difference, don't keep the extra complexity.

In Wokwi, add the DHT sensor library and PubSubClient libraries from the Library Manager tab. Wokwi has a virtual Wi-Fi network called Wokwi-GUEST with no password, and it is connected to the real internet, so the messages really do reach broker.hivemq.com. For a quick test, temporarily change 60ULL on line 31 to 6ULL so the board wakes up every minute. Click the DHT22 in the simulator to change the temperature and humidity. To see the messages, subscribe to your topic in an MQTT client (such as the one you used in lesson 5.1).

#1 T=24.0 C H=40.0 % Sent: {"t":24.0,"h":40.0,"n":1} Awake 3712 ms, fails=0. Sleep 10 min ... #2 T=24.0 C H=40.0 % Sent: {"t":24.0,"h":40.0,"n":2} Awake 1480 ms, fails=0. Sleep 10 min

The numbers above are only examples and will be different on your network, but notice the pattern: the first boot (with a full Wi-Fi scan) takes longer, and the later boots (with the channel shortcut) are shorter.

Why MQTT and not a web server?

A web server (lesson 4.2) has to stay awake all the time in case someone opens the page, which clashes with deep sleep. With MQTT, the device "pushes" its message whenever it wakes up and then leaves. Battery-powered devices are almost always "senders", not "servers".

Coach's advice

In battery projects, "I think" is banned; only "I measured" counts. For every change you make, write down two numbers: the sleep current (with a multimeter) and the awake time (with millis() just before sleeping). If the numbers didn't improve, undo the change. Anyone who works out battery life from the datasheet without measuring ends up, a week later, with a flat battery and an angry customer. And one more thing: don't be careless with lithium batteries. No project is worth a fire.

Your deep sleep code is correct, but the DevKit board draws 9 mA while asleep. What is the most likely reason?

  1. The regulator, the USB-to-serial converter and the LED on the development board
  2. The ESP32 never went to sleep
  3. Wi-Fi stayed on during sleep
  4. You declared too many RTC_DATA_ATTR variables

  2. EXT1 with ESP_EXT1_WAKEUP_ANY_LOW and the buttons to GND

  1. Because Wi-Fi interferes with reading the DHT22
  2. So that if the reading fails, we don't waste Wi-Fi's expensive energy

Exercises

Exercise 1: your own energy budget

Run the DeepSleep_Timer program on your board and measure the awake and sleep currents with a multimeter. Then use the average-current formula to work out the life of a 1000 mAh battery for "one 3-second wake-up every 5 minutes". Do it once with your own measured number and once with the datasheet number (10 µA). How many times different are they?

Hint

One cycle = 300 seconds. I_avg = (I_awake × 3 + I_sleep × 297) ÷ 300. Use the same units everywhere: convert everything to mA (10 µA = 0.01 mA). Then divide the capacity by the result, and report 75 percent of it as the realistic figure.

What should you see?

On the multimeter (wired as in the measuring section): while the board is awake, tens of milliamps (roughly 30 to 60 mA with Wi-Fi off; the last digits jump around). During the 10 seconds of sleep it drops to a few milliamps on a DevKitC (usually somewhere from 1 to 15 mA, depending on the board), never to 10 µA. DeepSleep_Timer is awake for only about half a second, which is too short for most meters: for this measurement only, change delay(500) to delay(3000).

Numbers to check your arithmetic against, worked out with example readings of 45 mA awake and 6 mA asleep (put your own readings in the same formula):

Sleep currentI_avgLife on paper (1000 mAh)Realistic (× 0.75)
Datasheet0.01 mA0.46 mAabout 2170 h ≈ 90 daysabout 68 days
Measured (example)6 mA6.39 mAabout 157 h ≈ 6.5 daysabout 4.9 days

Ratio: 6.39 ÷ 0.46 ≈ 14 times.

  • Your "measured" life must come out shorter than the datasheet one. If it's the other way round, you mixed up µA and mA.
  • With the datasheet number, the sleep term (0.01 × 297 ≈ 3) is tiny next to the awake term (I_awake × 3). With your measured number, the sleep term is the biggest one.
  • On a DevKitC the ratio usually lands somewhere between about 3 and 40 times. Far outside that range? Check the multimeter range and the units again.
Full answer with explanation

A worked solution with the example readings (45 mA awake, 6 mA asleep). Swap in your own numbers; the steps stay the same.

  1. One set of units. Everything in mA and seconds: 10 µA = 0.01 mA.
  2. One cycle. Every 5 minutes = 300 s: 3 s awake + 297 s asleep. (Using 300 s of sleep is a common slip: the awake seconds are part of the same cycle.)
  3. Datasheet sleep current: I_avg = (45 × 3 + 0.01 × 297) ÷ 300 = (135 + 2.97) ÷ 300 ≈ 0.46 mA. Life = 1000 ÷ 0.46 ≈ 2170 h ≈ 90 days; realistic: 0.75 × 2170 ≈ 1630 h ≈ 68 days.
  4. Measured sleep current: I_avg = (45 × 3 + 6 × 297) ÷ 300 = (135 + 1782) ÷ 300 ≈ 6.39 mA. Life = 1000 ÷ 6.39 ≈ 157 h ≈ 6.5 days; realistic: 0.75 × 157 ≈ 117 h ≈ 4.9 days.
  5. Ratio: 6.39 ÷ 0.46 ≈ 14. The same code, the same battery, 14 times shorter life, only because of the parts on the board that never sleep.
  6. What it teaches: on a DevKitC the sleep current decides everything. Even if the awake time were zero, the average would still be 6 mA. The fix is hardware (a low-power regulator, no always-on LED or USB chip), exactly the "hard truth" section of this lesson.

Grading: the cycle is 300 s with 297 s of sleep; all currents in mA; both lives reported, with the 0.75 factor; the ratio worked out; and one sentence on why they differ.

Exercise 2: a wireless doorbell

Write a program in which the board stays in deep sleep and wakes up from the button on GPIO27 (EXT0), connects to Wi-Fi, sends the message ding to your MQTT topic and goes back to sleep. If Wi-Fi doesn't connect within 8 seconds, it goes back to sleep without sending.

Hint

Take the connectWiFiFast() function and the MQTT part from the data logger. Instead of the timer, use esp_sleep_enable_ext0_wakeup(GPIO_NUM_27, 0) and the RTC pull-up. Remember to wait for the button to be released before sleeping: while (digitalRead(27) == LOW) delay(10);

What should you see?

In the Serial Monitor (the Awake times depend on your network and will differ):

Power-on: nothing to send
Awake 38 ms. Sleeping until the button...

rst:0x5 (DEEPSLEEP_RESET),boot:0x13 (SPI_FAST_FLASH_BOOT)
...

  • In your MQTT client, subscribed to your topic: exactly one ding per press.
  • Hold the button down for 3 seconds: still only one ding, and the board doesn't wake again until you press again.

Full answer with explanation 
// Wireless doorbell: sleep, wake on the GPIO27 button, send "ding", sleep again
#include <WiFi.h>
#include <PubSubClient.h>
#include "driver/rtc_io.h"

const char* WIFI_SSID = "Wokwi-GUEST";               // at home: your network name
const char* WIFI_PASS = "";
const char* TOPIC     = "esp32fa/yourname/doorbell"; // make it unique
WiFiClient net;
PubSubClient mqtt(net);
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint32_t rings = 0;                    // survives deep sleep

void sleepNow() {
  pinMode(27, INPUT_PULLUP);
  while (digitalRead(27) == LOW) delay(10);          // wait until the button is released
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_OFF);
  esp_sleep_enable_ext0_wakeup(GPIO_NUM_27, 0);      // wake when GPIO27 goes LOW
  rtc_gpio_pullup_en(GPIO_NUM_27);
  rtc_gpio_pulldown_dis(GPIO_NUM_27);
  Serial.printf("Awake %lu ms. Sleeping until the button...\n", millis());
  Serial.flush();
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  if (esp_sleep_get_wakeup_cause() != ESP_SLEEP_WAKEUP_EXT0) {
    Serial.println("Power-on: nothing to send");
    sleepNow();                                      // no "ding" when the battery goes in
  }
  rings++;
  Serial.printf("Button! Ring #%lu\n", (unsigned long)rings);
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);
  uint32_t t0 = millis();
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED && millis() - t0 < 8000) delay(20);
  if (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) {
    Serial.println("Wi-Fi timeout: not sent");
    sleepNow();                                      // never wait forever
  }
  mqtt.setServer("broker.hivemq.com", 1883);
  String id = "doorbell-" + WiFi.macAddress() + "-" + String(esp_random() & 0xFFFF, HEX);   // unique: MAC + random
  if (mqtt.connect(id.c_str()) && mqtt.publish(TOPIC, "ding")) {
    mqtt.loop();
    delay(50);                                       // a moment for the packet to leave
    mqtt.disconnect();
    Serial.println("Sent: ding");
  } else {
    Serial.println("MQTT failed: not sent");
  }
  sleepNow();
}

void loop() {}
  1. Settings, the MQTT objects and a ring counter. rings lives in RTC memory, so it keeps counting across deep sleep. The counter is your proof in the Serial Monitor: one press, one wake-up, one number higher. A normal variable would be 1 every time, because the program starts from the beginning after each wake-up.
  2. A sleep function that waits for the button to be released. It makes the pin an input with a pull-up so it can be read, waits for the release, switches Wi-Fi off, sets up EXT0 with the RTC pull-up, prints how long the board was awake, and sleeps. Without the waiting loop, a button held a little too long wakes the board again immediately and sends a second "ding". Printing millis() gives you the number that decides battery life.
  3. Send only if the button woke us. On power-up (any other wake-up cause) we go straight back to sleep. This way, putting the battery in doesn't ring the bell by itself.
  4. Count the ring and connect to Wi-Fi, for at most 8 seconds. If it doesn't connect in time, we sleep without sending. "Never wait forever": with the router switched off, an endless loop would empty the battery in one night.
  5. Connect to MQTT and publish ding. Thanks to &&, the publish only runs if the connection worked. Then mqtt.loop(), a 50 ms pause and a clean disconnect, as in the data logger. A client ID from the MAC address plus a random number stays unique even when many students test in Wokwi at once. Without the short pause, the packet may still be in the queue when the board falls asleep. Change the topic to one with your own name in it.
  6. Back to sleep, whatever happened. A battery device doesn't stay awake to "try again"; the next press tries again.

In Wokwi, add PubSubClient in the Library Manager tab, press ▶ and press the button after Sleeping until the button.... The virtual Wokwi-GUEST network is connected to the real internet, so the ding really reaches broker.hivemq.com and your MQTT client.

Exercise 3: cutting the sensor's power during sleep

Even in standby, the DHT22 draws a few tens of microamps, which is not small next to the ESP32's 10 microamps. Design a way to power the DHT22 only while the board is awake. What part do you need, and how must the code's timing change?

Hint

A transistor (or, for a very small current, a GPIO pin itself, after checking the pin's current limit) controls the sensor's VCC. Read the "holding a pin's state" section again. The DHT22 datasheet says you must wait a while after power is applied; that time adds directly to the awake time. Work out whether the trade is worth it.

What should you see?

With the answer sketch on a real board (set SLEEP_SEC to 10 for testing), every wake-up prints one line; the temperature and humidity are yours, and the "sensor on" time is a little over 2000 ms:

#1  T=23.8 C  H=41.2 %  (sensor on 2008 ms)
ets Jun  8 2016 00:22:57
rst:0x5 (DEEPSLEEP_RESET),boot:0x13 (SPI_FAST_FLASH_BOOT)
...
#2  T=23.8 C  H=41.3 %  (sensor on 2008 ms)
  • Never DHT22 read failed. If you get it every time, the wait after power-on is too short, or the pull-up resistor still goes to 3V3 instead of GPIO26.
  • Multimeter on volts between GPIO26 and GND: 3.3 V for about 2 seconds while the board is awake, 0 V the whole time it sleeps.
  • Your calculation should reach the same verdict as the answer: with wake-ups every 10 minutes, a plain 2-second wait awake costs more than it saves; waiting in light sleep, or waking up less often than about every half hour, makes it worth it.

This one is for a real board: Wokwi's light sleep isn't reliable (see the light sleep section).

Full answer with explanation

The part. For the DHT22, none: according to its datasheet it draws at most about 1.5 mA while measuring, far below what a GPIO pin can supply. So a GPIO pin can simply be its power supply. We use GPIO26, the course's pin for switching things (the same pin as in "holding a pin's state"). For a sensor that draws more than about 10 mA (a GPS module, many gas sensors), a pin isn't enough: use a P-channel MOSFET or a PNP transistor as a switch in the positive supply line, driven by the same pin.

The wiring. DHT22 VCC → GPIO26; DATA → GPIO13; GND → GND; and the 10 kΩ pull-up resistor from DATA to GPIO26, not to 3V3. If the pull-up stayed on 3V3, current would flow through the DATA pin into the switched-off sensor and half-power it, which wastes exactly the current you wanted to save.

The timing. Every wake-up now starts with a "cold" sensor. After switching its power on, wait about 2 seconds (the datasheet asks for 1 to 2) before reading. Switch it off right after reading, and lock GPIO26 LOW during deep sleep with gpio_hold_en.

Is it worth it? With wake-ups every 10 minutes (600 s):

  • Saving: the DHT22's standby current, about 0.05 mA (50 µA), disappears for the whole cycle: −0.05 mA on average.
  • Cost if you wait awake (about 40 mA without Wi-Fi) for 2 s: 40 × 2 ÷ 600 ≈ 0.13 mA. The cost is bigger than the saving: not worth it.
  • Cost if you wait in light sleep (0.8 mA): 0.8 × 2 ÷ 600 ≈ 0.003 mA. Net saving ≈ 0.047 mA: worth it.
  • Break-even for the awake wait: 40 × 2 ÷ 0.05 = 1600 s ≈ 27 minutes. If you wake up less often than about every half hour, even the simple wait pays off.
  • And on a DevKitC that sleeps at several milliamps, 0.05 mA is lost in the noise; this trick only matters on a low-power board.

The sketch below keeps only the sensor part of the data logger, so you can see the timing clearly. To send the reading, put the logger's Wi-Fi and MQTT part after the reading, unchanged.

// DHT22 powered from GPIO26: sensor power only while the board is awake
#include <DHT.h>

const int PIN_DHT_PWR = 26;              // feeds the DHT22's VCC (at most 1.5 mA)
const int PIN_DHT     = 13;
const uint64_t SLEEP_SEC = 600;          // every 10 minutes (10 while testing)
DHT dht(PIN_DHT, DHT22);
RTC_DATA_ATTR uint32_t bootCount = 0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  bootCount++;
  gpio_hold_dis((gpio_num_t)PIN_DHT_PWR);   // release the lock from the last sleep
  pinMode(PIN_DHT_PWR, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(PIN_DHT_PWR, HIGH);          // sensor power ON
  uint32_t tOn = millis();
  dht.begin();
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(2000000ULL); // the DHT22 needs about 2 s after power-on:
  esp_light_sleep_start();                   // wait in light sleep (0.8 mA), not awake

  float t = dht.readTemperature();
  float h = dht.readHumidity();
  digitalWrite(PIN_DHT_PWR, LOW);           // sensor power OFF right after reading
  pinMode(PIN_DHT, INPUT);                  // no internal pull-up feeding the sensor
  if (isnan(t) || isnan(h)) Serial.printf("#%lu  DHT22 read failed\n", (unsigned long)bootCount);
  else Serial.printf("#%lu  T=%.1f C  H=%.1f %%  (sensor on %lu ms)\n",
                     (unsigned long)bootCount, t, h, (unsigned long)(millis() - tOn));

  gpio_hold_en((gpio_num_t)PIN_DHT_PWR);    // keep GPIO26 LOW during deep sleep
  gpio_deep_sleep_hold_en();
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(SLEEP_SEC * 1000000ULL);
  Serial.flush();
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void loop() {}
  1. Pins, sleep time and a wake-up counter. GPIO26 is the sensor's power supply; the DHT22's data stays on GPIO13. A 10-minute cycle, like the data logger. While testing, set 10 seconds so you don't wait an hour for six lines.
  2. Release the lock, then switch the sensor on. The pin is locked LOW from the last sleep, so first gpio_hold_dis, then output HIGH. We note the time so we can print how long the sensor was on. Without releasing the lock first, digitalWrite does nothing and the sensor never gets power (the trap from the "holding a pin's state" section).
  3. The 2-second wait, in light sleep. A 2-second timer, then esp_light_sleep_start(). The program carries on at line 21 afterwards, with all its variables. This is the line that decides whether the trick pays off: 2 s at 0.8 mA instead of 2 s at about 40 mA. Nothing is printed before it, so no text gets cut off by the nap. GPIO26 keeps its HIGH level during light sleep.
  4. Read, then switch the sensor off straight away. GPIO13 goes back to a plain input without the internal pull-up. The sensor is powered for only about 2 seconds per cycle. The internal pull-up on GPIO13 would otherwise feed a little current into the switched-off sensor, the same problem as a pull-up on 3V3.
  5. Print the result and how long the sensor was on. "sensor on 2008 ms" proves the wait happened; nan tells you the wait is too short or the wiring is wrong.
  6. Lock GPIO26 LOW and go into deep sleep. Without the lock the pin may float during sleep and the sensor could get power again. GPIO26 is an RTC pin, so gpio_hold_en alone would do; line 30 does no harm (see the pin-hold section).
The lesson in three lines

Battery life = capacity ÷ average current, and the average current is set by the sleep current and the awake time. Deep sleep (about 10 µA for the chip) restarts the program from setup and keeps only RTC_DATA_ATTR; light sleep (about 0.8 mA) carries on from the same line. A development board draws several milliamps asleep: always measure with a multimeter, and use lithium batteries only with a protected charger and a low-power regulator.
