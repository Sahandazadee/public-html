What is a pin?

Every metal leg along the edge of the board is a pin: a point where an outside wire connects to the ESP32's internal circuitry. Most pins are GPIO (General Purpose Input/Output): your program decides whether the pin "talks" (output: it supplies a voltage) or "listens" (input: it reads a voltage). The remaining pins are for power (3V3, 5V, GND) or control (EN).

An everyday example: the doors of a building

Think of the ESP32 as an office building with 38 doors. Some doors work both ways for everyone (the safe GPIOs). Some are entrance-only and can't be opened from the inside (34 to 39). Some are emergency doors: if someone happens to be standing in front of them when the building opens in the morning, the whole building opens in the wrong mode (strapping). And six doors belong to a private storeroom that is locked and must never be touched (flash). Knowing the pinout means knowing which kind each door is.

One pin, three names: the biggest source of confusion

A pin can be referred to in three different ways, and mixing them up is beginners' number-one mistake:

Naming schemeExampleWhere you see itUsed in code?

The "D2" trap: one name, two completely different pins

On the DOIT DevKit V1 board (30 pins), the pin labeled D2 is GPIO2; on that board "D" is just a prefix and the number after it is the GPIO number.

But on the DevKitC board (38 pins), the pin labeled D2 at the bottom of the left row is SD2 = GPIO9, one of the flash memory pins! If someone follows a DOIT tutorial and connects a wire to "D2" on a DevKitC, the board crashes. In the same way, D3 on the DevKitC means GPIO10, D0 means GPIO7 and D1 means GPIO8.

(To complete the confusion: on ESP8266 boards such as the NodeMCU, D2 means GPIO4! Now you see why you should always think in GPIO numbers.)

The golden rule of pin numbering

In code, write only the GPIO number: digitalWrite(4, HIGH). Don't use constants like D4 in ESP32 code; on most ESP32 boards they either aren't defined at all or mean something different. Before wiring, match the printed label against the official pinout of your own board.

Power pins: 3V3, 5V, GND and EN

Do the math: the 3V3 current budget

Suppose your board's regulator can supply about 600 mA (the common AMS1117 regulator, under good conditions and if it stays cool). With Wi-Fi on, the ESP32 draws up to about 250 mA during transmit bursts. That leaves about 350 mA for everything else:

OLED about 20 mA + DHT22 about 1.5 mA + three LEDs at 6 mA each (18) ≈ 40 mA. Perfectly safe.

But an SG90 servo draws a few hundred milliamps while moving, and even more when it stalls, and a 30-LED color strip can draw up to about 1800 mA. These must never be powered from 3V3. If they are, the voltage sags, the ESP32 prints Brownout detector was triggered and keeps resetting.

No two power sources at once

While the USB cable is plugged in, don't connect another external supply to the 5V or 3V3 pin (unless you have a dedicated protection circuit). Two supplies at slightly different voltages can "fight", and current can flow back into your computer's USB port. Simple rule: one board, one supply. Only the GNDs must always be shared. (The right ways to power a circuit from a battery or an external supply are in lesson 1.4.)

Digital input and output, pull-up and pull-down

Every ordinary GPIO has two modes:

  • Output (OUTPUT): with digitalWrite you set the pin HIGH (about 3.3 V) or LOW (0 V).
  • Input (INPUT): with digitalRead you ask whether the voltage on the pin is high (HIGH) or low (LOW). Rough thresholds: above about 2.5 V reads as HIGH and below about 0.8 V reads as LOW (75% and 25% of 3.3 V).

Floating pins and the pull-up resistor

An input pin that isn't connected to anything is floating: like a tiny antenna, it picks up mains noise and the electric charge from your hand, and reads randomly HIGH and LOW. If you put a button between the pin and GND, the pin floats whenever the button isn't pressed. The fix is a pull-up resistor: a large resistor (usually 10 kΩ) between the pin and 3.3 V that gently "pulls" the pin HIGH by default. When the button is pressed, the direct connection to GND wins and the pin goes LOW.

An everyday example: a door spring

A pull-up is like a door spring that always keeps the door closed. If someone pushes the door (the button), it opens; when they let go, the spring closes it again. Without the spring, the door swings with every breeze and you never know whether it's open or closed. A pull-down is the same spring pulling in the opposite direction (toward GND).

Luckily, most ESP32 GPIOs have internal pull-up and pull-down resistors of about 45 kΩ that you switch on with a single word: pinMode(27, INPUT_PULLUP) or pinMode(27, INPUT_PULLDOWN). The exception: pins 34 to 39 (next section).

The current limit of each pin

Numbers to memorize

The absolute maximum current a GPIO can supply is about 40 mA; that's the "burn-out line", not the "everyday limit". Our recommendation: at most 20 mA per pin (and the total across all pins is limited too). An LED with a 220 Ω resistor draws about 6 mA, which is perfect. But never connect a motor, relay, coil, large buzzer or LED strip directly to a GPIO. The pin only gives the "command"; a transistor or driver module draws the real current from the power supply.

Input-only pins: 34, 35, 36 (VP), 39 (VN)

These four pins are input-only in hardware. They have no output circuitry, so:

  • They can't light an LED or output a PWM signal. pinMode(34, OUTPUT) doesn't cause a compile error, but it does nothing.
  • They have no internal pull-up or pull-down. pinMode(35, INPUT_PULLUP) doesn't connect any resistor and the pin keeps floating. If you connect a button to one of them, always add an external 10 kΩ resistor.
  • They are all on ADC1, so they're excellent for reading analog sensors (potentiometer, LDR, soil moisture sensor), even with Wi-Fi on.
Why this limitation is a blessing

Because these pins aren't good for anything else, they're the best home for analog sensors and they keep the general-purpose pins free for outputs. Good habit: put your analog sensors on 34, 35, 36 and 39 first, then spread out the rest. The names VP and VN come from "Sensor Voltage Positive/Negative"; for you they're simply GPIO36 and GPIO39.

ADC1 and ADC2: why Wi-Fi breaks analog input

An ADC (analog-to-digital converter) turns a voltage from 0 to about 3.3 V into a number from 0 to 4095 (12 bits). The ESP32 has two separate ADCs:

ConverterPins (on the DevKitC)With Wi-Fi on
ADC132, 33, 34, 35, 36, 39✅ Works
ADC20, 2, 4, 12, 13, 14, 15, 25, 26, 27❌ Doesn't work (error or meaningless number)
Why? A shared kitchen

ADC2 is like a kitchen you share with your roommate. Your roommate (the Wi-Fi driver) has set a rule: "Whenever I'm home, the kitchen is mine." The Wi-Fi driver uses the ADC2 hardware for its own work and won't lend it to your program as long as Wi-Fi is on. ADC1 is your private kitchen and is always available to you.

A common true story

Someone connects a potentiometer to GPIO4, and in a simple program the readings are perfect. Then they add Wi-Fi so they can see the number on their phone, and suddenly analogRead always returns zero or a fixed number. There's nothing wrong with the code; GPIO4 is simply on ADC2. Moving one wire to GPIO34 and changing one number in the code fixes the problem.

Two more things about the ADC

1) The ESP32's ADC isn't perfectly linear: it's inaccurate near 0 V and near the top of its range (above about 3.1 V). For a more accurate number, use analogReadMilliVolts(pin) instead of converting by hand; it applies the factory calibration. 2) Never feed more than 3.3 V straight into an ADC input; bring it down with a voltage divider. Full details are in lesson 3.1.

DAC: only GPIO25 and GPIO26

A DAC is the opposite of an ADC: it turns a number into a real voltage. The ESP32 has two 8-bit DACs: GPIO25 (DAC1) and GPIO26 (DAC2). With dacWrite(25, 128), about half of 3.3 V (about 1.65 V) appears on the pin; the number ranges from 0 to 255. It's handy for simple sound and reference voltages. (Remember that the S3 and C3 have no DAC at all?)

DAC versus PWM

PWM (lesson 3.2) switches a pin on and off very fast and raises or lowers the "average" voltage; it works on any output pin. A DAC produces a truly steady in-between voltage, but only on two pins. For dimming an LED or driving a servo, PWM is enough and the better choice.

Touch pins: T0 to T9

Ten GPIOs can sense the touch of a finger without any extra parts: you just connect a wire or a piece of foil to the pin. Your body acts like a small capacitor, and when you touch the wire, the pin's capacitance changes. The touchRead(pin) function returns a number that, on the original ESP32, gets smaller when touched.

Strapping pins: settings read at power-up

At the moment the ESP32 powers up or resets, before running even a single line of your program, it looks once at the voltage on five pins and makes important decisions based on them: "Should I run the program, or wait for a new one?", "What voltage should I power the flash memory with?". These are called strapping pins. After boot they work like ordinary GPIOs; the problem is only at the moment of power-up.

An everyday example: switches under the desk

Imagine an office worker who, every morning on arrival, glances at a few switches under the desk to decide what to do that day: "If the first switch is down, today I only answer the phone." After that first glance, they forget about the switches. If you accidentally left your bag on one of those switches the night before, their whole day starts out wrong. A part you connect to a strapping pin is that "bag on the switch".

Three real failure stories

Story 1: "My board won't boot with the sensor, but boots without it" (GPIO12)

A module with a built-in pull-up resistor (for example a button module or some sensor modules) is connected to GPIO12. At power-up it pulls GPIO12 HIGH; the ESP32 thinks the flash is a 1.8 V part and powers it with the wrong voltage. The Serial Monitor repeatedly shows messages such as flash read err, 1000. Disconnect the wire and everything works again. Fix: move the part to a green pin.

Story 2: "The program doesn't run, it just says waiting for download" (GPIO0)

A relay module, or a button connected to GND, sits on GPIO0 and holds it LOW at power-up. In the Serial Monitor you see boot:0x3 (DOWNLOAD_BOOT...) and waiting for download. The board is fine; it's just waiting for a new program. Fix: leave GPIO0 free for the BOOT button.

Story 3: "Uploading fails with an LED on GPIO2" (GPIO2)

On some boards and circuits, whatever is connected to GPIO2 holds it high when the upload starts, and the upload tool fails with A fatal error occurred: Failed to connect to ESP32. The board's own built-in blue LED on GPIO2 is designed so that it causes no trouble; the problem comes from external parts with a pull-up. Fix: disconnect the GPIO2 wire while uploading, or move the part.

For pros: GPIO12 can be "neutralized", but don't

With the espefuse tool you can permanently lock the flash voltage to 3.3 V so that GPIO12 no longer matters. This is irreversible (a one-time-programmable fuse), and if your module has a 1.8 V flash, the board is ruined for good. For a beginner the simplest approach is: don't use GPIO12.

Forbidden pins: GPIO6 to GPIO11 (flash)

These six pins are connected inside the module to the flash memory, the place where your program is stored and from which the processor reads instructions all the time. On the DevKitC they are printed as CLK, D0, D1, D2, D3, CMD (or SD0 to SD3). If you connect anything to them or use them in code, the processor's link to the flash is disrupted and the board immediately crashes or resets.

An absolute rule

Connect no wire at all to GPIO6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11, and don't use them in code either. Not even for "just one quick test". On the 30-pin DOIT board these pins aren't brought out at all, which is itself a safety bonus.

UART0: GPIO1 (TX) and GPIO3 (RX)

These two pins are connected to the USB converter; every Serial.print message and the program upload itself go through them. If you connect a part to them, either the Serial Monitor breaks, or uploads fail, or your part receives the boot messages and behaves strangely. GPIO1 also sends data out during boot, and GPIO3 is HIGH during boot. Use them only when you've truly run out of pins and no longer need Serial. If you need a second serial port, use Serial2 on GPIO16 and GPIO17 and write the pins explicitly: Serial2.begin(9600, SERIAL_8N1, 16, 17). If you write only Serial2.begin(9600), core version 3 uses GPIO4 and GPIO25 by default, not 16 and 17.

Why stick to the defaults?

Because every library example and every tutorial on the internet assumes these pins. Put the OLED on 21 and 22 and the example code works unchanged. Put it on other pins and you have to write Wire.begin(SDA, SCL) with your own pins; that's possible, but it's one more place to make a mistake.

RTC pins: waking up from deep sleep

In deep sleep, most of the chip switches off and only the small, low-power RTC section stays awake. Only the pins connected to this section can wake the ESP32 with a button or a sensor:

0, 2, 4, 12, 13, 14, 15, 25, 26, 27, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 39

So if you want a battery-powered project to wake up at the press of a button, put the button on one of these pins (preferably the safe ones: 4, 13, 25, 26, 27, 32, 33, or the input-only pins with an external resistor). Pins such as 16, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22 and 23 are not RTC pins and can't wake the board from deep sleep. Details in lesson 6.1.

Pins that "glitch" at boot

Some pins, in that fraction of a second at power-up and before your program runs, automatically go HIGH or output a PWM signal:

  • GPIO1 (sends the boot messages) and GPIO3 (HIGH during boot)
  • GPIO5, GPIO14 and GPIO15 (PWM signal during boot)
  • GPIO6 to GPIO11 (flash activity; they're off-limits anyway)
Relay danger: the garage door opens for a moment!

Imagine a relay that controls a garage door motor or a water pump is connected to GPIO14. Every time the power drops and comes back, or the board resets, GPIO14's brief PWM burst switches the relay on for an instant, and the motor gets a kick. On an LED you'd never notice this glitch, but on a power device it can be dangerous. Rule: put anything that "moves" or "switches heavy power" (relay, motor, solenoid valve, MOSFET) on a green pin, and preferably add an external 10 kΩ pull-down resistor on its driver's input so it's guaranteed to stay off at power-up.

How is the 30-pin DOIT board different?

The GPIO numbers and their capabilities are exactly the same, because the module is the same WROOM-32. The board simply doesn't bring out eight of the pins:

  • GPIO6 to GPIO11 are missing (great: it saves you from the red mistake).
  • GPIO0 isn't brought out as a pin; you reach it only through the BOOT button.
  • It has one fewer GND.

The usual pin order on the DOIT (with the USB port facing down; the printing on your own board always has the final say): first column EN, VP, VN, D34, D35, D32, D33, D25, D26, D27, D14, D12, D13, GND, VIN and second column D23, D22, TX0, RX0, D21, D19, D18, D5, TX2, RX2, D4, D2, D15, GND, 3V3. Remember: TX2 = GPIO17, RX2 = GPIO16, TX0 = GPIO1, RX0 = GPIO3, and VIN is the same as 5V.

Experiment: touch and an input-only pin

Now let's try out what you've learned, hands-on: a touch wire on GPIO32 (T9), which is both touch-capable and safe (in the course pin plan it's one of the swappable pins, and the touch wire is only there for this experiment), and a potentiometer on GPIO34, which is input-only and on ADC1.

// Touch on GPIO32 and a potentiometer on the input-only pin GPIO34
const int TOUCH_PIN = 32;          // T9: touch-capable and safe
const int POT_PIN   = 34;          // input-only, ADC1 (works with Wi-Fi too)
int touchThreshold;                // touch threshold; worked out on your own board in setup

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(500);
  // no pinMode needed: touchRead and analogRead set up the pin themselves
  int idle = touchRead(TOUCH_PIN);   // the "untouched" value (don't touch the wire at power-up!)
  touchThreshold = idle * 2 / 3;     // about two thirds of it = the touch boundary
  Serial.printf("Touch + input-only pin test (idle=%d, threshold=%d)\n", idle, touchThreshold);
}

void loop() {
  int touchValue = touchRead(TOUCH_PIN);           // smaller number = touched
  int potRaw     = analogRead(POT_PIN);            // 0 to 4095
  int potMv      = analogReadMilliVolts(POT_PIN);  // calibrated millivolts

  Serial.printf("touch=%d  pot=%d  (%d mV)\n", touchValue, potRaw, potMv);

  if (touchValue < touchThreshold) {
    Serial.println(">> touched!");
  }
  delay(300);
}
  1. Choosing pins for a reason. GPIO32 is touch channel T9 and in the green table; GPIO34 is input-only and on ADC1. This is exactly the "pin-selection checklist" in action: we spent the input-only pin on an analog input (it can't do anything else anyway) and chose ADC1 so the readings won't break if Wi-Fi is added later.
  2. The touch threshold variable. The number below which a reading counts as a "touch". Here we only create the variable; its value is filled in during setup. The touchRead value depends on the wire length, the size of the foil, the humidity, the board itself and even the version of the Arduino-ESP32 core (it might be about 60 on one board and several hundred on another). A fixed number like 30 that you saw in some tutorial might never detect a touch on your board.
  3. setup without pinMode. We open Serial and wait half a second. Line 9 is just a comment. touchRead and analogRead put the pin into the right mode (touch or analog) on their own. If you had written pinMode(34, INPUT_PULLUP), no pull-up would be connected, because pins 34 to 39 have no internal pull-up at all.
  4. Automatic calibration. We read the touch value once "hands off", set the threshold to two thirds of it, and print both numbers. Because a touch lowers the number on the original ESP32, anything below about two thirds of the resting value means a touch. This way the program works on any board and any core version without tweaking. The only condition is that you don't touch the wire at power-up or when you press EN.
  5. Reading touch. touchRead measures the pin's capacitance. When your finger touches the wire, the number drops. On the original ESP32, "lower means touched". (On the S2 and S3 it's the other way round: touching makes the number bigger. If you ever move this code to an S3, flip the condition.)
  6. Two ways to read the potentiometer. analogRead gives a raw number from 0 to 4095; analogReadMilliVolts gives the voltage in millivolts with the factory calibration applied. The raw number is fine for relative comparisons (for example "the knob is turned halfway"). When you need the real voltage, the millivolt version is more accurate, because the ESP32's ADC isn't perfectly linear.
  7. Printing one complete line. printf prints three numbers neatly on one line. One line per pass of loop keeps the Serial Monitor (and the Serial Plotter) readable, so you can easily follow the changes.
  8. Deciding with if. If the touch value is below the threshold, the touched message is printed. This is the simplest form of "sensor → decision → action". Later, instead of a message, you can switch on an LED or a relay (on a green pin!).
  9. A 300 ms pause. We measure about three times per second. Any faster and the Serial Monitor fills up with numbers you can't read; any slower and the reaction to a touch feels sluggish.

Sample output (the touch numbers on your board will differ; depending on the board and the core version they may be in the hundreds. All that matters is the drop in the number when you touch):

Touch + input-only pin test (idle=58, threshold=38) touch=58 pot=0 (142 mV) touch=57 pot=2047 (1650 mV) touch=11 pot=2049 (1651 mV) >> touched! touch=56 pot=4095 (3150 mV)

Look closely at the last line: the potentiometer is turned all the way, the raw number is 4095, but the millivolt reading is about 3150, not 3300. That's the ADC's "non-linear ceiling" we mentioned earlier. Likewise, at the bottom of the range (potentiometer at zero), the millivolt reading may not be exactly zero.

(Run it in Wokwi with the same three steps as in lesson 1.1.) In Wokwi you can fully test the potentiometer part: turn the knob with your mouse and watch the numbers. Try the touch part on a real board; the simulator has no real finger, and its touch number has no physical meaning.

The pin-selection checklist (for all your future projects)

Every time you start a new project, before connecting even one wire, go through these 8 steps on paper, in order:

  1. Cross out the reds. Remove GPIO6 to GPIO11 from the list. Set GPIO1 and GPIO3 aside for Serial as well.
  2. Place the protocols first. I2C on 21 and 22, SPI on 23, 19, 18 and 5, Serial2 on 16 and 17 with the pins written explicitly (if you need it). Don't spend these pins on anything else.
  3. Put analog inputs on ADC1. First 34, 35, 36 and 39 (input-only), then 32 and 33. If the project uses Wi-Fi, never ADC2.
  4. Put power outputs (relay, motor, MOSFET) on green pins with no boot glitch: for example 4, 13, 25, 26, 27, 32, 33. Never 1, 3, 5, 14 or 15.
  5. Buttons: on green pins with INPUT_PULLUP. If you put one on 34 to 39, add an external 10 kΩ resistor. If it has to wake the board from sleep, pick an RTC pin.
  6. Touch: 4, 13, 27, 32, 33. DAC: only 25 and 26.
  7. Strapping pins are the last resort. If you must use one, use it only for a simple output such as an LED (GPIO2 is fine for an LED), and never for something that sets its voltage at power-up. Leave GPIO12 free whenever you can.
  8. Work out power and current. At most 20 mA per pin; power-hungry parts get their power from 5V or a separate supply (with a shared GND); never more than 3.3 V on any GPIO.
Coach's advice

I ask my students to write a "pin table" before every circuit: first column the part, second column the GPIO, third column "why this pin?". If your only answer for the third column is "because it was free", you're not ready to wire yet. Write this table at the top of your code as a comment, too. Six months from now, when you open the project again, you'll thank yourself. And one more thing: never say "I'll just test 5 V for a second". ESP32 pins don't know what "a second" is.

The course pin plan: a fixed seat for every part

Now let's run the checklist once, for the whole course. Instead of choosing new pins in every lesson, we give every part that stays on your breadboard its own fixed GPIO, like assigned seats in a classroom: everyone always knows where to find everyone, and nobody sits in someone else's chair. Once a part is wired, it stays put from lesson to lesson, and you never have to wire it again.

Swappable pins: one seat, several guests

Some parts only show up in one or two lessons. They share three "swappable" pins; before you plug in a new part, you take the previous guest off:

  • GPIO32: a servo's signal, or the data line of an addressable (NeoPixel) LED strip.
  • GPIO26: a relay, a motor driver input, a MOSFET gate or a stepper driver's STEP input. It has no boot glitch, so the load stays off at power-up.
  • GPIO14: a second motor or stepper input. GPIO14 briefly outputs a PWM signal at boot (see above); for a motor driver's input that's a harmless twitch of a few milliseconds, but never put a relay on it.

A few exceptions are explained where they happen: the deep-sleep examples (chapter 6), the examples that port code to the S3 and C3 chips, the ESP32-CAM (which has its own fixed pins), and the built-in LED on GPIO2 when a lesson is talking specifically about the board's own LED.

  • If you watched this video in lesson 1.2, watch it now with new eyes: whenever a board's pinout is on screen, pause and find the strapping, input-only and flash pins on it.
  • Notice which capabilities stay the same across boards (original ESP32, S3, C3) and which change. The rules in this lesson apply only to the original ESP32; the S3 and C3 have different strapping maps.
  • Recommended further reading: the ESP32 Pinout Reference on Random Nerd Tutorials and the official GPIO page in the ESP-IDF documentation.

Lesson quiz (important: get them all right)

You want to read a potentiometer and show its value on your phone over Wi-Fi. Which pin is best?

  1. GPIO4
  2. GPIO34
  3. GPIO25
  4. GPIO12
GPIO34 is on ADC1 (it works with Wi-Fi), and because it's input-only it isn't useful for anything else, so this is its best use.

You've connected a button between GPIO36 and GND and written pinMode(36, INPUT_PULLUP). The button randomly reads as "pressed". Why?

  1. Because GPIO36 is on ADC2
  2. Because GPIO36 is a strapping pin
  3. Because GPIO36 is connected to the flash
  4. Because GPIO36 has no internal pull-up resistor and the pin is floating
Pins 34, 35, 36 and 39 have no internal pull-up or pull-down. INPUT_PULLUP does nothing on them; you need an external 10 kΩ resistor.

You've connected a sensor module whose output has a pull-up to GPIO12, and now the board won't boot. What happened?

  1. GPIO12 was HIGH at boot, so the ESP32 powered the flash with 1.8 V (the wrong voltage)
  2. The board went into download mode
  3. The sensor erased the flash memory
  4. ADC2 is clashing with the sensor
GPIO12 (MTDI) is the strapping pin for the flash voltage. HIGH at boot = 1.8 V, but the WROOM module has a 3.3 V flash, so the flash can't be read.

On the 38-pin DevKitC board, which GPIO is the pin labeled D2?

  1. GPIO2
  2. GPIO4
  3. GPIO9 (a flash pin; forbidden)
  4. GPIO22
On the DevKitC, D2 means SD2 = GPIO9, which is connected to the flash. That's why you should always work with GPIO numbers, in your code and in your head, and match the printed label against your own board's pinout.

You want to control a water pump relay. Which pin is more suitable?

  1. GPIO14
  2. GPIO26
  3. GPIO15
  4. GPIO1
GPIO26 is green and doesn't glitch at boot. For power outputs, always pick the quiet green pins and add an external pull-down.

Which group of pins should you never use?

  1. 34, 35, 36, 39
  2. 0, 2, 5, 12, 15
  3. 21 and 22
  4. 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11
GPIO6 to GPIO11 are connected to the flash memory. The processor reads the program from the flash all the time; any interference = a crash.

You have a battery-powered project that sits in deep sleep and must wake up at the press of a button. Which pin do you connect the button to?

  1. GPIO18
  2. GPIO22
  3. GPIO33
  4. GPIO23
Only RTC pins (such as 4, 13, 25 to 27, 32, 33, 34 to 39) can wake the board from deep sleep. GPIO33 is both an RTC pin and green.

An LED with a 68 Ω resistor is connected to GPIO4 (LED voltage drop about 2 V). Is it safe?

  1. No; about 19 mA, right at the recommended 20 mA limit with no safety margin; a larger resistor (for example 220 Ω) is better
  2. Yes, it's only about 2 mA
  3. No, it's 48 mA and the pin burns out instantly
  4. No, because GPIO4 can't be an output
(3.3 − 2) ÷ 68 ≈ 0.019A = 19mA. That's below the absolute limit of 40 mA, but right at the recommended 20 mA. With 220 Ω you get about 6 mA: completely safe and still bright.

Exercise 2: calibrating touch

Run the Touch_And_InputOnly program on a real board. Write down ten touch readings without touching and ten while touching, and work out the average of each. Where does the program's automatic threshold (threshold in the first line of output) fall between those two numbers? If a touch is sometimes missed, change the 2 / 3 factor (for example to 3 / 4). Then make the foil bigger (say, the size of your palm), press the EN button and repeat the experiment. What changes?

Hint

Usually the untouched number is a few dozen, and a touch brings it down to less than half. Bigger foil has more capacitance, so the untouched number also drops; in other words, the threshold needs to be set again. A very long wire also increases noise.

Exercise 3: pin detective (no code)

Your friend built this circuit and says "nothing works right". Find every mistake and suggest a replacement pin for each: an analog soil moisture sensor on GPIO26 (with Wi-Fi), a pump relay on GPIO15, a button on GPIO39 with INPUT_PULLUP, an LED on GPIO35, and a module with a pull-up output on GPIO12.

Answer

1) GPIO26 is on ADC2; it doesn't work with Wi-Fi → GPIO34 or 32. 2) GPIO15 outputs PWM during boot and is a strapping pin; the pump switches on for an instant → GPIO26 with a pull-down (the relay's seat in the course pin plan). 3) GPIO39 has no internal pull-up → either an external 10 kΩ resistor, or move the button to GPIO27 with INPUT_PULLUP. 4) GPIO35 is input-only, so the LED won't light → GPIO4. 5) GPIO12 HIGH at boot = the board doesn't boot → a green pin such as GPIO13.

The lesson in three lines

In code, always work with GPIO numbers and match your board's printed labels against the pinout; never touch 6 to 11, keep 1 and 3 free for Serial, and never give any pin more than 3.3 V or more than 20 mA. Analog inputs go on ADC1 (32 to 39), because ADC2 stops working with Wi-Fi; 34 to 39 are input-only and have no pull-up. Leave the strapping pins (0, 2, 5, 12, 15) free at boot, put power parts on green pins with no boot glitch, and run the 8-step checklist on paper before every project.
