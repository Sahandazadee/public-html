Electricity in 5 minutes: voltage, current, resistance

Before we even mention the ESP32, you need to understand three words. Without them, every safety warning in this course (such as "never put 5 volts on a pin" or "don't draw more than 20 milliamps") is just a sentence to memorize. With them, you'll understand the why yourself.

An everyday example: the water pipes in a building

Picture a water tank on the roof, connected by a pipe to the kitchen tap.

Voltage is like water pressure: the higher the tank, the harder the water pushes when it reaches the tap. Its unit is the volt (V).
Current is like the amount of water that flows through the pipe each second. Its unit is the ampere (A); in small electronics we usually work in milliamps (mA), which are thousandths of an amp.
Resistance is like how narrow the pipe is: a narrow pipe holds the water back. Its unit is the ohm (Ω).

Ohm's law: how the three are related

These three ideas are tied together by one simple formula called Ohm's law: V = I × R. In words: voltage equals current times resistance. If you know any two of the three numbers, you can work out the third: I = V ÷ R or R = V ÷ I.

Do the math: three worked examples

Example 1. We connect a 1000-ohm resistor (1 kilohm, 1kΩ) to 3.3 volts. How much current flows? I = 3.3 ÷ 1000 = 0.0033A = 3.3mA.

Example 2. Connect the same resistor to 5 volts: I = 5 ÷ 1000 = 5mA. More voltage = more pressure = more current.

Example 3. If the resistance is almost zero (say, a bare wire between 3.3 volts and ground), I = 3.3 ÷ 0 heads toward infinity. In practice the regulator or battery delivers as much current as it possibly can, the wire gets hot and a part burns out. This is called a short circuit, and it's the number one reason beginners burn out boards.

Power: why parts get hot

There is a fourth number too: power, measured in watts (W). It means "how much energy is used, or turned into heat, every second". The formula is P = V × I. For example, with Wi-Fi on, the ESP32 draws about 150 milliamps from 3.3 volts: P = 3.3 × 0.15 ≈ 0.5W, or half a watt. A household LED bulb uses about 9 watts, so the ESP32 uses roughly eighteen times less power than a light bulb. That low power draw is exactly what makes it a good fit for battery-powered devices.

Why learn this now?

Because the ESP32 is a sensitive part that runs on 3.3 volts, and each of its pins can only handle a few tens of milliamps. Any mistake with voltage or current means a burnt board. Someone who understands Ohm's law does the math before connecting a wire; someone who doesn't is left wondering after the board burns out.

What does ground (GND) mean?

Voltage is always measured relative to some point, just as we measure height relative to the floor. In a circuit we call this reference point ground, or GND, and treat its voltage as zero. When we say "the pin is at 3.3 volts", we mean "3.3 volts above GND". That's why, in every circuit in this course, the GND of every part must be connected together.

What is a microcontroller, and how is it different from a computer?

Your computer or phone has a powerful processor that can't do anything on its own: it needs separate RAM, a separate hard drive or SSD, a separate network card and an operating system (Windows, Android) to manage them all. The result: huge power, but also high power consumption, a high price, and anywhere from a few seconds to tens of seconds to start up.

A microcontroller (MCU) is a complete but tiny computer on a single chip: the processor, the memory and the "hands and feet" (pins for lighting an LED or reading a button or sensor) all sit in one part the size of a fingernail. It has no heavy operating system; it has just one program, which starts the moment it powers up and keeps repeating as long as there is power.

An everyday example: the office manager and the night guard

A computer is like the manager of a big office: they know how to do a thousand different things and have a secretary, a filing room and a switchboard, but it takes them half an hour every morning to get settled in, and they're paid a lot.

A microcontroller is like a night guard: they have just one instruction on a sheet of paper ("check the door every ten minutes; if it's open, sound the alarm"), they start work the moment they arrive, never get tired, and are paid very little. For simple, repetitive jobs, the guard is a better fit than the manager.

Microcontrollers are everywhere, and you deal with dozens of them every day: in the washing machine, the TV remote, your mouse, the air-conditioner thermostat, smart lights and even an electric toothbrush.

What exactly is the ESP32?

The ESP32 is a microcontroller released in 2016 by the Chinese company Espressif Systems (based in Shanghai). What made it famous is that it has Wi-Fi and Bluetooth built into the chip itself, while still being very cheap. Before it came along, getting an Arduino onto the internet meant buying a separate module and dealing with a lot of wiring and hassle.

Why two cores?

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth have time-critical jobs: they must answer radio packets on time. If your program happens to be busy in a long loop at that moment, the connection drops. In Arduino-ESP32, by default, most of the radio work runs on core 0 and your loop() function runs on core 1, so both jobs get along side by side more easily.

Peripherals are like specialist staff

The processor is the boss, but it doesn't do every job itself. For example, when you want to fade an LED smoothly, it tells the PWM unit "switch this pin on and off in this ratio" and moves on to its next task; the PWM unit keeps doing that job on its own, forever. This division of labor is what makes the ESP32 so capable despite its small size.

The ESP32 in real products

The ESP32 isn't just a learning toy. Millions of commercial devices are built on it:

  • Smart switches and plugs: for example the Shelly Plus series and many other Wi-Fi switches that you control from a phone app.
  • Smart lights and LED strips: the well-known open-source project WLED runs on the ESP32.
  • Home sensors: thermometers, humidity sensors, air-quality sensors and water-leak detectors that send their data to your phone.
  • Farming and industry: soil-moisture monitoring, remote control of water pumps, industrial counters.
  • Low-cost cameras: the ESP32-CAM board (lesson 5.3) for simple security cameras and video doorbells.

In other words, the skills you learn in this course are the same skills companies use to build real products.

Why did we choose the ESP32 for this course?

Because for most "smart home" and "Internet of Things" projects it has the best balance: it's more than ten times faster than an Arduino Uno and has 260 times more RAM, it gets Wi-Fi and Bluetooth for free, and unlike a Raspberry Pi it boots in a second, runs for months on a battery and isn't damaged when power is cut. Board anatomy: where each part is and what it does

The ESP32 itself is a small square chip, and soldering it is a job for specialists. That's why we always buy it as a development board: a ready-made board that puts the chip, memory, antenna, USB converter and voltage regulator together, with legs that plug straight into a breadboard. The most official board is the ESP32-DevKitC V4, made by Espressif itself:

  1. (1) 5V Power On LED. A small red LED that lights up while the USB cable is connected. It only tells you power has arrived; your program can't control it. If it doesn't light, check the USB cable or port first.
  2. (2) I/O Connector (the rows of pins). Two rows of 19, for 38 pins in total (if your board is a DOIT DevKit V1, it has two rows of 15, so 30 pins; you'll see the difference in lesson 1.2). Every pin has a printed name (such as 23, GND, 3V3). Every connection to the outside world goes through here. Lesson 1.3 is entirely about these pins.
  3. (3) The ESP32-WROOM-32E module. The silver metal box. Inside it are the ESP32 chip itself, 4 MB of flash memory, a 40 MHz crystal (the heartbeat of the processor's clock) and the radio parts. The metal shield keeps radio noise out. The black part sticking out on the right (with no metal shield) is the antenna: a zigzag copper trace on the board that sends and receives Wi-Fi and Bluetooth waves.
  4. (4) USB-to-UART Bridge. A small square chip; on this board it's a CP2102 made by Silicon Labs. The original ESP32 doesn't speak USB; it only understands the serial language (UART). This chip translates between your computer's USB and the module's UART. Cheaper boards usually have a CH340 instead, which needs a different driver (lesson 2.1).
  5. (5) BOOT button. Connected to the GPIO0 pin. If you hold it down while the board powers up or resets, the ESP32 waits to receive a new program instead of running the old one (download mode). Most of the time the Arduino IDE does this automatically; if an upload gets stuck, this button is your lifesaver.
  6. (6) micro-USB port. It takes 5 V power from the computer, carries the program to the board, and sends Serial messages back to the computer. Three jobs, one cable.
  7. (7) EN (reset) button. Connected to the EN pin. Pressing it means "switch off and on again": the program starts over from the beginning (from setup()). The program is not erased.
The part without a number: the 3.3 V regulator

In the photo above, next to the CP2102 chip, you'll see a black three-legged part with a wide metal tab. This is the voltage regulator (LDO). Its job is to turn the 5 volts from USB into a steady 3.3 volts, because 5 volts would burn out the ESP32. It's like a pressure-reducing valve in a building's plumbing: it turns high incoming pressure into a safe, steady pressure. The extra energy turns into heat, which is why this part gets a little warm; that's normal. Anything you connect to the 3V3 pin also draws its power from this regulator, so its capacity is limited (usually a few hundred milliamps for the whole board).

Where is the blue LED?

The official DevKitC board only has the red power LED and no programmable LED. But most of the inexpensive boards on the market (such as the DOIT DevKit V1) also have a blue LED on GPIO2. If your board has two LEDs, red means power and blue means "yours to control from your program". You'll get to know the different kinds of boards in lesson 1.2.

A closer look at the WROOM module

If we pulled off the module's metal shield (which you shouldn't!), we'd see the module's own pins. The image below is a top view of the WROOM module's solder pads. The development board simply connects these pads to bigger, easier-to-use pins.

Keep the area under the antenna clear

The antenna radiates radio waves into the air. If there is metal, wire, a battery or your hand under or over it, the board may get a weak Wi-Fi signal. When you design an enclosure or a circuit, always let the antenna stick out past the edge and never run wires underneath it.

The big danger: 3.3 volts versus 5 volts

This is the most important hardware warning in the whole course, so read it carefully. The Arduino Uno runs on 5 volts, and most older tutorials on the internet and most modules on the market are designed for 5 volts. But the ESP32's pins can only handle up to 3.3 volts. The absolute maximum in the datasheet is about 3.6 volts.

An everyday example: the garden hose and the thin pipe

Imagine your bathroom plumbing is designed for normal city water pressure, and someone hooks an industrial pump up to it. The pipes might survive the first moment, but the seals slowly burst. An ESP32 pin is exactly the same: 5 volts may not burn it out instantly, but inside the chip there are small protection diodes that soak up the extra current until they overheat and fail. The result is often a "slow death": the pin starts acting a little strange at first, then stops working for good.

With numbers: connecting a 5 V output to an ESP32 pin

Say you've connected a 5 V sensor's output straight to an ESP32 pin. The pin's internal protection diode steers anything above about 3.3 + 0.6 = 3.9 volts into the 3.3 V line. The difference, 5 − 3.9 = 1.1V, has to drop across the only resistance in the path: the very small resistance of the sensor's output and the wires. By Ohm's law, a very small resistance means a very large current. A diode built for a few milliamps can't survive tens of milliamps.

The simple fix: a voltage divider made of two resistors (for example 10 kΩ and 20 kΩ) that brings 5 volts down to about 5 × 20 ÷ 30 ≈ 3.3V, or a logic level shifter module. You'll build the divider and measure it with a multimeter, and see how a level shifter is used, in lesson 1.4, and we'll remind you again wherever it matters in the chapter 3 lessons.

What is firmware, and how does it get onto the board?

Without a program, the ESP32 is just a piece of silicon. The program, or firmware, is the list of instructions that tells the processor what to do. It's called "firmware" because it sits somewhere between hardware (hard) and software (soft): it's stored in the device's own flash memory and isn't erased when the power goes off.

But the ESP32 doesn't understand English (or Persian); it only understands machine language (ones and zeros). We write the program in C++ (with the simplifying Arduino libraries), and another program called a compiler translates it into machine language. Here is the full path:

  1. Write. You write the code in an editor (such as the Arduino IDE) on your computer. At this stage the board doesn't even need to be connected.
  2. Compile. When you click the check-mark button (✓), the compiler checks your code. If there is a typo (say, a missing ;), it stops right here with a red error message. If everything is correct, it builds a binary file.
  3. Upload. When you click the arrow button (→), a tool called esptool sends the binary file to the board over USB through the USB-to-serial converter (CP2102 or CH340). At this moment the board automatically enters download mode (with help from the USB converter's control lines), which is the same thing the BOOT button does by hand.
  4. Save to flash. The program is written into flash memory, replacing the previous program. From now on, even if you unplug the board from the computer and plug it into a phone charger, this same program runs.
  5. Run. The board resets automatically and the new program starts from setup().

What does a real program look like?

You aren't going to upload anything yet (installing the tools is in lesson 2.1). But it's worth seeing what a program looks like right now. This little program asks the ESP32 to introduce itself and print, straight from the chip, the specs you just read about in the table above:

// Introduction program: the ESP32 introduces itself
void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);          // open the message channel to the computer
  delay(1000);                   // wait one second so the Serial Monitor is ready

  Serial.println("Hello! I am an ESP32.");
  Serial.print("Chip model: ");
  Serial.println(ESP.getChipModel());
  Serial.print("CPU cores: ");
  Serial.println(ESP.getChipCores());
  Serial.print("CPU speed (MHz): ");
  Serial.println(ESP.getCpuFreqMHz());
  Serial.print("Flash size (bytes): ");
  Serial.println(ESP.getFlashChipSize());
}

void loop() {
  Serial.println("I am alive...");   // one message every 2 seconds
  delay(2000);
}
  1. A comment. Everything after // is for humans to read only; the compiler ignores it. A program without comments becomes a puzzle, even for the person who wrote it, a month later.
  2. The setup() function. Everything between this function's curly braces { and } runs only once, after power-up or a reset. Preparation jobs (like opening the message channel) are only needed once. Every Arduino program must have both setup and loop, even if they are empty.
  3. Start serial communication and wait a moment. Serial.begin(115200) opens a message channel over USB at 115,200 bits per second. delay(1000) means wait 1000 milliseconds. The ESP32 has no screen; Serial is our only way to see inside the program. The one-second wait helps make sure the first messages aren't lost before the Serial Monitor window opens.
  4. Print text and the chip model. println sends the text and moves to a new line; print doesn't move to a new line. ESP.getChipModel() asks the chip itself for its model name. Combining print and println builds one readable line: a label, then a value. It's a good habit to pick up right from the start.
  5. Number of cores, speed and flash size. Three more functions from the ESP object that return, in order, the number of cores (usually 2), the processor speed in MHz (usually 240) and the flash size in bytes (usually 4194304, which is 4 MB). With this program you can actually measure your own board's specs instead of trusting what the seller wrote. In lesson 1.2 we build on this same idea to identify the type of board.
  6. The loop() function. After setup, this function repeats forever: every two seconds it prints an "I'm alive" message. A microcontroller has no operating system for the program to "return" to; the program never ends. Seeing this repeating message proves the board hasn't frozen.

The output in the Serial Monitor looks something like this (the exact chip model on your board may be slightly different):

Hello! I am an ESP32. Chip model: ESP32-D0WD-V3 CPU cores: 2 CPU speed (MHz): 240 Flash size (bytes): 4194304 I am alive... I am alive...

Before the first line you may see a few lines of boot messages (such as rst:0x1 (POWERON_RESET)) or a few garbled characters; that's normal and comes from the chip itself at the moment it powers up. If you only see I am alive... and the introduction lines are missing, setup finished before the Serial Monitor opened; press the EN button once to run the program from the start. (You'll try all this on a real board in lesson 2.2.)

You can run this program even without buying a board. Wokwi is a free simulator in your browser; think of it as a virtual ESP32 board that lives on your computer screen. Just three steps:

  1. Click "Open Wokwi" to open an empty ESP32 project in a new tab.
  2. In Wokwi, open the diagram.json tab, delete all of its contents and paste in the text you get with the "Copy diagram.json" button.
  3. Take the code with the "Copy sketch.ino" button, paste it over the old code in the sketch.ino tab and press the green ▶ button. The output appears in the Serial Monitor at the bottom.

In the simulator, the chip model and flash size may differ from a real board. The full Wokwi tutorial is in lesson 2.3.

How many languages can you use with the ESP32?

Besides Arduino (which we use in this course), you can also program the ESP32 with ESP-IDF (Espressif's official, professional toolkit in C) or MicroPython (the Python language). Arduino is the easiest place to start and has the most tutorials and libraries. In fact, the Arduino-ESP32 core is itself built on top of ESP-IDF, so nothing you learn goes to waste. Official sources: the Arduino-ESP32 documentation and the ESP32 datasheet.

In the water-pipe model, what does "voltage" correspond to?

  2. Water pressure (the height of the tank)
  3. How narrow the pipe is
  4. The color of the water
Voltage is like pressure: the force that pushes current along. Current is how much flows, and resistance is what holds the flow back.

  1. 1089 mA
  2. 1 mA
  3. 10 mA
  4. 100 mA
I = 3.3 ÷ 330 = 0.01A = 10mA. Ohm's law: current equals voltage divided by resistance.

Which statement about the original ESP32 is not true?

  1. It has two processor cores
  2. It has both Bluetooth Classic and BLE
  3. Its pins work at 3.3 volts
  4. It connects to 5 GHz Wi-Fi
The original ESP32 only has 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi. If your router only broadcasts on 5 GHz, the ESP32 can't see it.

What does the CP2102 chip do on the DevKitC board?

  1. It translates between the computer's USB and the ESP32 module's UART (serial)
  2. It turns 5 volts into 3.3 volts
  3. It stores the program
  4. It sends out the Wi-Fi waves
The original ESP32 doesn't speak USB; the CP2102 (or the CH340 on cheaper boards) is a USB-to-serial translator. That's why your computer needs its driver.

  1. Raspberry Pi 5
  2. ESP32
  3. Arduino Uno
  4. An old laptop
The ESP32 has built-in Wi-Fi and can drop into deep sleep, drawing only a few microamps, between readings. The ideal combination for a battery-powered device.

After a successful upload, you unplug the board from the computer and connect it to a phone charger. What happens?

  1. The program is erased and the board does nothing
  2. The board burns out because the charger has a different voltage
  3. The last program you uploaded starts running from setup
  4. The program waits for the computer
The firmware stays in flash memory. The computer is only needed to "write" the program, not to run it. (You just won't see the Serial messages anywhere anymore.)

Full answer with explanation

Use the same three steps every time: convert the resistance to ohms, work out I = V ÷ R (the answer is in amps), then multiply by 1000 to get milliamps.

  1. a) 10kΩ = 10 × 1000 = 10000Ω. So I = 3.3 ÷ 10000 = 0.00033A, and multiplied by 1000 that's 0.33mA. Completely safe; this is the typical value of a pull-up resistor.
  2. b) I = 3.3 ÷ 100 = 0.033A = 33mA. Above the recommended limit (20 mA) and close to the pin's absolute limit (about 40 mA). 100 Ω is far too little to put straight between a pin and GND.
  3. c) I = 1.3 ÷ 220 ≈ 0.0059A ≈ 5.9mA. Safe and just right for an LED: plenty of light, and well away from the pin's limit.

How to grade yourself: for each case, both the number and the decision (safe or not) must be right. The main point of the exercise: the smaller the resistance, the bigger the current; the resistor you put between a pin and a part is what keeps the pin alive.

Practice project: introduce your board

Every electronics product starts life on a workbench, and the first thing a maker does with a new board is ask it to say hello. Your job: make the ESP32 print a small "name card" with your name, its chip model and three Ohm's-law answers that the chip works out by itself. When it works, you have run your first real program and checked the maths from the start of this lesson with a computer.

Requirements

  1. The program opens Serial at 115200 and waits one second, just like the Hello_ESP32 example.
  2. It prints a title line and an Owner: line with your own name.
  3. It prints Chip model: followed by the model the chip reports about itself (ESP.getChipModel()).
  4. It prints three labelled Ohm's-law lines: the current through 1000 Ω at 3.3 V (in mA), the voltage when 0.005 A flows through 1000 Ω, and the resistance that lets 0.015 A flow at 3.3 V.
  5. The chip calculates each number itself: you type the formula (for example 3.3 / 0.015), never the answer.
  6. Everything is printed once, in setup(), and ends with a closing line; loop() stays empty.

Parts

  • Your ESP32 board and a USB data cable, or nothing at all: the Wokwi simulator runs this project in your browser.
  • No wiring: the program only talks over USB.

The skeleton already compiles: it opens Serial and prints the label of the first result. Your work is in the six TODO comments.

A line with a label and a value is built from three calls: Serial.print("label ");, Serial.print(value); and Serial.println(" unit");. Only the last one moves to a new line.

Hint 2

Hint 3

// Introduce your board: a name card plus three Ohm's-law results
void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);          // open the message channel to the computer
  delay(1000);                   // give the Serial Monitor a moment

  Serial.println("=== Hello! Meet my ESP32 ===");
  Serial.println("Owner: Alex");               // put your own name here
  Serial.print("Chip model: ");
  Serial.println(ESP.getChipModel());

  // Result 1: current, I = V / R (x 1000 turns amps into milliamps)
  Serial.print("I = 3.3 V / 1000 ohm = ");
  Serial.print(3.3 / 1000 * 1000);
  Serial.println(" mA");

  // Result 2: voltage, V = I x R
  Serial.print("V = 0.005 A x 1000 ohm = ");
  Serial.print(0.005 * 1000);
  Serial.println(" V");

  // Result 3: resistance, R = V / I
  Serial.print("R = 3.3 V / 0.015 A = ");
  Serial.print(3.3 / 0.015);
  Serial.println(" ohm");

  Serial.println("=== End of card. Press EN to print it again. ===");
}

void loop() {
  // nothing to repeat: the card is printed once, in setup
}
  1. Open the channel and wait. The same two lines as in Hello_ESP32. The card is printed once, so the Serial Monitor must be ready before the first line leaves the chip.
  2. Title, owner and chip model. Plain text with println, then a label with print and the model with println. A card with your name proves it's your program running; the chip model is read from the chip itself, so it can't lie.
  3. Result 1: current. 3.3 / 1000 gives the current in amps (0.0033); * 1000 turns it into milliamps. Serial.print of a number with a decimal point prints two decimals: 3.30. The chip does the maths from the formula you typed. Change 1000 to 220 and it instantly tells you the current through a 220 Ω resistor (15.00 mA).
  4. Result 2: voltage. 0.005 * 1000: 5 mA through 1 kΩ needs 5 volts. This is example 2 of the lesson worked backwards; getting the same 5 V is a good check that you understand V = I × R.
  5. Result 3: resistance. 3.3 / 0.015 = 220 Ω. 220 Ω is exactly the resistor you'll put in front of LEDs later in the course; now you know where that number comes from.
  6. Closing line and an empty loop. The last line says how to see the card again; loop has nothing to do. loop must exist even when it's empty, or the program won't compile.

The lesson in three lines

Voltage is pressure, current is how much flows, and resistance is what holds it back; they're all tied together by V = I × R. The ESP32 is a dual-core 240 MHz microcontroller with 520 KB of RAM, 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth; it runs on 3.3 volts, and 5 volts must never reach its pins. A program is written, compiled, uploaded over USB through a CP2102 or CH340 converter, and stored in flash, and it runs from setup every time the board powers up.
